Outsourcing RPAA compliance: what a PSP can hand over and what it keeps

Guide · Published 19 November 2026 · 7 min read · AML Canada

A payment service provider can outsource RPAA compliance work, such as writing its risk management and incident response framework, assessing risks and drafting notices, to an outside risk officer or adviser. The PSP keeps the legal duty: approval by its senior officer and board, the notices and filings, the records and the oversight of anyone it hands work to.

Key points

  • Section 17(1) of the RPAA puts the risk management and incident response framework on the PSP, and section 87 makes it liable for violations by its third-party service providers, agents and mandataries.
  • The senior officer and any board approve the framework under RPAR s. 5(6), and the PSP itself gives incident notices (RPAA s. 18) and submits the annual report (RPAA s. 21).
  • If the framework gives a role to an outside party, RPAR s. 5(5) requires it to say how the PSP oversees that party.
  • A PSP with an internal or external auditor needs an independent review under RPAR s. 10, done by someone with no role in the framework, so the adviser who maintains it can't do it.

Can a PSP outsource RPAA risk management?

Yes. A payment service provider (PSP) can use outside people to write, run and challenge parts of its risk management and incident response framework. It can't hand over the legal duty. Section 17(1) of the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) requires the PSP itself to establish, implement and maintain the framework.

The Bank of Canada's operational risk and incident response guideline (October 2024) allows for outside help. Paragraph 4.1.1 counts people provided by a third party as part of a PSP's human resources. Paragraph 3.3 says an external party, such as a consultant, can be responsible for challenging and overseeing roles in the framework. The Retail Payment Activities Regulations (RPAR) let the framework draw on resources "whether from internal or external sources" (s. 5(1)(c)).

The PSP stays answerable. The Bank's retail payments supervision FAQ says every PSP subject to the RPAA is responsible for ensuring compliance with the Act and its regulations. Section 87 of the Act makes a PSP liable for violations committed by its employees, third-party service providers, agents and mandataries acting within their role. As of 19 November 2026, the Justice Laws texts of the Act and the regulations are current to 29 October 2026.

What must stay with the PSP?

Five duties stay with the PSP whoever does the drafting:

  • Approval. Under RPAR s. 5(6), the senior officer approves the framework at least once a year and after each material change, and the board, if there is one, approves it at least once a year.
  • Senior officer oversight. Unless the PSP is an individual, RPAR s. 5(1)(d)(ii) makes a senior officer responsible for overseeing compliance and material decisions on operational risks and incidents.
  • Reports to the senior officer. Review findings (RPAR s. 8(4)), test records (s. 9(3)) and independent review gaps (s. 10(3)) go to the senior officer.
  • Notices and filings. Section 18 of the Act requires the PSP to notify the Bank and each materially affected end user, PSP or designated clearing house without delay. Section 21 requires the PSP to submit the annual report.
  • Records and access. RPAR s. 40 requires records sufficient to show compliance, kept until five years after they stop showing current compliance. Section 42 keeps records held by agents and third-party service providers accessible to the PSP, and section 17(3) of the Act requires the PSP to give the Bank the documents and data it asks for in an assessment.

What each framework must contain is covered in our guide to RPAA compliance frameworks.

Can an outside risk officer be the PSP's senior officer?

Only if the person meets the definition in section 1 of the RPAR. A senior officer is a board member who is also a full-time employee; the CEO, COO, CFO, chief risk officer or another listed officer, or someone performing similar functions; or any other officer who reports directly to the board, CEO or COO.

The Bank's FAQ says that, because of that last branch, a senior officer need not be a direct employee of the PSP, provided they report directly to the board, CEO or COO. The PSP should assess whether the person has the capabilities, including seniority and authority, to fulfil the role.

Choosing the senior officer is the PSP's decision. Under our risk officer scope, the PSP's team approves the framework at board level.

How must a PSP oversee an outside risk officer?

If the framework gives any role or responsibility to a third party, RPAR s. 5(5) requires it to set out the systems, policies, procedures, processes, controls or other means the PSP uses to oversee how that party carries it out. Paragraph 3.13 of the guideline says this should include who conducts due diligence and who monitors delivery of the services and performance of the roles.

Whether an outside adviser is also a third-party service provider under section 2 of the Act depends on the contract and the nature of the services, the Bank's FAQ says. Paragraph 12.1.2 of the guideline says each PSP must assess which of its providers are in scope. For those in scope, the yearly and pre-contract assessments in RPAR s. 5(3) apply.

Paragraph 3.3 also asks PSPs to respect separation of duties where necessary and feasible, so that one individual doesn't control a process from start to end. Our scope splits the work this way: we design the framework, assess the risks, review and advise, and the PSP's team runs the operations.

Who can carry out the independent review?

The independent review must be done by a sufficiently skilled individual who has had no role in establishing, implementing or maintaining the framework. RPAR s. 10(1) requires a PSP with an internal or external auditor to have that person review the framework at least once every three years. The review covers whether each element meets section 5 and whether the PSP complies with sections 6 to 9.

That rules out the outside risk officer who writes and maintains your framework. Paragraph 11.4 of the guideline says the reviewer may be a PSP employee or an external party, provided they meet those conditions. Under paragraph 11.2, a PSP "has an external auditor" when it regularly uses one for independent assurance; ad hoc use for specific testing or reviews doesn't count.

The PSP must obtain a record of the reviewer's name, or the reviewing entity's name, with the date, scope, methodology and findings (RPAR s. 10(2)). It reports gaps, vulnerabilities and the measures taken to the senior officer (s. 10(3)). Under our scope, we prepare the framework documentation for the reviewer, and the PSP appoints the reviewer and acts on the findings.

What to settle before you outsource

Write the outside role into the framework itself, because RPAR s. 5(1)(d) requires the framework to allocate roles and responsibilities. Before the engagement starts, check that:

  • the framework names the outside risk officer's role and the means of overseeing it (RPAR s. 5(5));
  • you have named a senior officer who meets the section 1 definition and has the seniority and authority the Bank's FAQ describes;
  • you have assessed whether the adviser is a third-party service provider and, if so, covered it in your section 5(3) assessments;
  • the records the adviser works on stay accessible to you and are kept for the period in RPAR s. 40;
  • someone inside the PSP gives the notices and files the reports, since the Act puts those duties on the PSP;
  • if you have an internal or external auditor, the independent reviewer is someone other than the adviser who maintains the framework.

Outsourcing RPAA risk management: common questions

Still not sure? Book a consultation or write to info@amlcanada.ca.

Can a PSP outsource risk management under the RPAA?

It can outsource the work, and the Bank's guideline counts people provided by a third party as part of a PSP's human resources. The legal duties stay with the PSP: section 17(1) of the RPAA requires it to maintain the framework, and section 87 makes it liable for violations by its third-party service providers, agents and mandataries.

Can an outside consultant be the PSP's senior officer?

Only if they meet the definition in section 1 of the RPAR. The Bank's FAQ says a senior officer need not be a direct employee if they report directly to the board, CEO or COO, and the PSP should assess their seniority and authority.

Can the adviser who wrote our framework do the independent review?

No. RPAR s. 10(1) requires a sufficiently skilled individual who has had no role in establishing, implementing or maintaining the framework. The review is required at least every three years for PSPs with an internal or external auditor.

Is an outside risk officer a third-party service provider?

It depends on the contract and the services. The RPAA defines a third-party service provider as one that provides a service related to a payment function under a contract. Either way, RPAR s. 5(5) requires the framework to set out how the PSP oversees any third party given a role in it.

Who files incident notices and the annual report if risk work is outsourced?

The PSP. Section 18 of the RPAA requires the PSP to give incident notices and section 21 requires it to submit the annual report. Under our scope, we draft the notices and the annual report, and the PSP's team sends and files them.

The RPAA compliance frameworks a PSP needs

Every payment service provider needs a written risk management and incident response framework, and one that holds end-user funds also needs a safeguarding-of-funds framework. Third parties, agents and the senior officer's role are built into those two documents. Record keeping rules cover both, and the annual report, change notices and incident notices draw on them.

RPAA incident reporting and the 48-hour rule

When a payment service provider becomes aware of an incident with a material impact, it must notify the Bank of Canada and the affected end users, payment service providers and clearing houses without delay. The Bank expects the first notice no later than 48 hours after the provider decides the incident is material.

RPAA safeguarding of end-user funds

Since September 8, 2025, a payment service provider that holds funds for its users must safeguard them, either in trust in a trust account or in a segregated account backed by insurance or a guarantee. It also needs a written safeguarding framework and an independent review at least every three years.
