RPAA business continuity plan: recovery and testing for PSPs

Guide · Published 19 November 2026 · 5 min read · AML Canada

An RPAA business continuity plan is the recovery part of the risk management and incident response framework every payment service provider must keep. The regulations require a response and recovery plan, measurable reliability targets and a testing methodology with a record of each test. This guide covers what the plan contains, how and how often to test it, and what to keep.

Key points

  • Continuity and recovery sit inside the risk management and incident response framework under RPAA s. 17(1) and RPAR s. 5.
  • RPAR s. 9 sets no fixed test frequency: your methodology sets it, and you must test before any material change.
  • Each test needs a record of its date, methodology, results and actions, sent to the senior officer if there is one.
  • Tests must take into account your reliance on third-party service providers.

What must an RPAA business continuity plan cover?

The Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) has no stand-alone business continuity rule. Continuity and recovery sit inside the risk management and incident response framework that section 17(1) requires of every payment service provider (PSP). Section 5 of the Retail Payment Activities Regulations (RPAR) sets out the contents.

The continuity parts are:

  • an objective of performing retail payment activities without reduction, deterioration or breakdown, including keeping systems and data available (s. 5(1)(a)(i));
  • measurable reliability targets and indicators (s. 5(1)(b));
  • business continuity and resilience risks and their causes (s. 5(1)(f)(i));
  • a response and recovery plan that covers incidents involving agents, mandataries or third-party service providers (s. 5(1)(i));
  • manual processes or alternate solutions if primary systems fail, and how quickly they start (s. 5(1)(i)(ii));
  • how you will confirm the status of all transactions, recover lost or corrupted data and correct integrity issues (s. 5(1)(i)(vii)).

Section 5(2) requires all of it to be proportionate to the impact a breakdown could have on end users and other PSPs. Our guide to RPAA compliance frameworks covers the rest of the framework, and our guide to RPAA incident reporting covers notices to the Bank.

What recovery objectives does the Bank of Canada recommend?

Paragraph 2.7 of the Bank's Operational risk and incident response guideline (October 2024) recommends that more ubiquitous or interconnected PSPs set, at a minimum, system availability targets, recovery time objectives, maximum tolerable downtimes and recovery point objectives. Every PSP still needs measurable reliability targets under RPAR s. 5(1)(b).

Appendix B defines them:

  • A system availability target is the percentage of time a system or service should be fully available.
  • A recovery time objective is the maximum time, set by the PSP, within which a disrupted system or service should resume.
  • A maximum tolerable downtime is the recovery time objective plus the work recovery time needed to verify data and bring it up to date.
  • A recovery point objective is the maximum data loss, measured in time, you will accept.

What is business continuity testing?

Under RPAR s. 9(1), business continuity testing is part of a testing methodology every PSP must establish and implement. Its purpose is to find gaps in the effectiveness of, and vulnerabilities in, the systems, policies, procedures, processes and controls in its risk management and incident response framework.

The methodology must be proportionate (s. 9(1)(a)) and cover high-likelihood and high-impact risks (s. 9(1)(b)). Its tests must involve internal stakeholders, including agents, mandataries and decision-makers, and take into account reliance on third-party service providers (s. 9(1)(c)). It must set the frequency and scope of testing (s. 9(1)(d)) and test before any material change (s. 9(1)(e)).

Paragraph 10.7 of the guideline expects three categories of test: verifying and validating controls, scenario-based testing, and testing of changes.

How do you test a business continuity plan?

You test a business continuity plan by running exercises on scenarios drawn from your own identified risks, then checking whether people, backups and workarounds perform as written. Appendix G of the Bank's guideline lists methods: documentation review, interviews, sample-based testing, desktop reviews, tabletop exercises, walk-throughs and simulations.

Appendix G says incident response plan tests should cover:

  • escalation channels and decision-making arrangements;
  • whether staff can follow the plan and return to normal operations;
  • whether backups work and backed-up data can be reliably retrieved;
  • any manual workarounds or alternative processing the plan relies on.

Scenarios should be based on known operational risks, change as operations develop, and come with a documented rationale, according to Appendix G. The Canadian Centre for Cyber Security's ITSAP.10.005 is general guidance and not an RPAA requirement. It names seminars, tabletop exercises and live simulations as ways to test a plan.

How often should a business continuity plan be tested?

As of 19 November 2026, the RPAR sets no fixed number of tests per year. Section 9(1)(d) makes your own testing methodology set the frequency and scope of testing. Section 9(1)(e) adds a test before you adopt any material change to the framework's systems, policies, procedures, processes or controls, or to operations that affect them.

Paragraph 10.4 of the guideline asks for a risk-based approach that tests more important systems more often and in more depth. Section 8(1) of the RPAR requires a framework review at least once a year, so an exercise before each review gives it current results.

Securities regulators set yearly cycles for some of their own sectors. The OSC's Staff Notice 11-764 (2011) notes that National Instrument 21-101 requires marketplaces to test their plans at least annually. That rule does not apply to PSPs.

What to record and how results feed the framework review

RPAR s. 9(2) requires a record of each test showing its date, methodology, results and the measures taken or planned in response. Section 9(3) requires the record to go to the senior officer, if you have one. Paragraph 10.20 of the guideline adds the scope tested and your reasons for leaving any gap unremedied.

Section 8(2)(b) requires the framework review to evaluate how well you met your objectives against your targets and indicators. Appendix F of the guideline lists lessons learned from testing, and the status of resulting actions, among the review's sources. Section 40 requires records to be kept until five years after they stop showing current compliance.

How do third-party providers fit into a continuity plan?

RPAR s. 5(1)(i)(i) requires your response and recovery plan to take into account each third-party service provider's incident response procedures and the need to coordinate with them. Section 9(1)(c)(ii) requires tests that take into account your reliance on those providers.

  • Paragraph 10.14 of the guideline suggests testing assumptions about whether a provider's services will be available.
  • Appendix H lists a provider's business continuity and disaster recovery arrangements, and its testing of them, among the practices to assess.
  • Paragraph 12.26 lists adding a provider's services to your testing methodology as a compensating control.

Paragraph 10.6 lets you use testing done for other purposes if it meets the objectives of section 9. What you can hand to a provider is covered in our guide to RPAA outsourcing risk management.

RPAA continuity and testing: common questions

Still not sure? Book a consultation or write to info@amlcanada.ca.

Does the RPAA require a business continuity plan by that name?

No. RPAR s. 5 requires a response and recovery plan, reliability targets and the identification of business continuity and resilience risks inside the risk management and incident response framework.

Does a PSP have to test its plan every year?

The RPAR sets no fixed frequency. Your testing methodology sets it under s. 9(1)(d), and s. 9(1)(e) requires a test before any material change.

Who has to see the test results?

RPAR s. 9(3) requires the record of each test to go to the senior officer, if the PSP has one.

Can testing done for another purpose count?

Yes, if it meets the objectives of section 9, according to paragraph 10.6 of the Bank's guideline. If its scope falls short, run additional tests.

Are Cyber Centre or OSC documents binding on PSPs?

No. The binding rules are the RPAA and the RPAR. The Bank of Canada's guideline sets out its supervisory expectations for meeting them.

RPAA incident reporting and the 48-hour rule

When a payment service provider becomes aware of an incident with a material impact, it must notify the Bank of Canada and the affected end users, payment service providers and clearing houses without delay. The Bank expects the first notice no later than 48 hours after the provider decides the incident is material.

PSP insolvency: what happens to end-user funds in Canada

In a PSP insolvency in Canada, the end-user funds a payment service provider holds are meant to be paid to end users as soon as feasible, because section 20 of the RPAA requires those funds to be held in trust or covered by insurance or a guarantee. Whether that works depends on the provider's ledger, its shortfall checks and its written procedures for returning funds.
