What is the RPAA?

Guide · Updated 11 October 2026 · 3 min read · AML Canada

The Retail Payment Activities Act is the federal law under which the Bank of Canada supervises payment service providers. It sets rules on operational risk, incidents and the safeguarding of users' funds.

Key points

  • The RPAA applies to payment service providers performing retail payment activities.
  • Foreign PSPs are covered when they direct activities at Canada and serve end users here.
  • Banks, credit unions and some other institutions are excluded.
  • The RPAA sits alongside FINTRAC rules, and many businesses need both.

What the RPAA is

The Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) brought payment service providers (PSPs) under federal supervision for the first time. Payment processors, digital wallets and money transfer apps are typical examples. The Bank of Canada supervises them, with a focus on three things: managing operational risk, responding to incidents, and protecting the funds PSPs hold for their users.

The Act defines a PSP as an individual or entity that performs payment functions as a service or business activity that isn't incidental to another service or business activity. A retail payment activity is a payment function performed in relation to an electronic funds transfer in Canadian or another currency.

The five payment functions

The Act lists five payment functions. A PSP performs one or more of them for end users:

  • keeping an account for one or more end users that's used for electronic funds transfers;
  • holding funds for an end user until they're withdrawn or sent to someone else;
  • starting an electronic funds transfer when an end user asks for it;
  • authorizing an electronic funds transfer, or transmitting, receiving or facilitating the instruction for one;
  • clearing or settlement services.

Who the Act covers

The RPAA applies to retail payment activities performed by a PSP with a place of business in Canada. It also applies to a PSP outside Canada that directs retail payment activities at individuals or entities in Canada and performs them for end users in Canada (RPAA section 5).

A new PSP must register with the Bank before it performs any retail payment activities, according to the Bank's registration criteria. Registered PSPs are listed on the Bank's public registry.

What's excluded

Sections 6 to 10 of the Act set out exclusions. Some institutions are outside the RPAA, including banks, authorized foreign banks, provincially regulated credit unions and caisses populaires, insurance companies, federal trust and loan companies, and provincially regulated trust companies.

Some activities are excluded too, including payments made with closed-loop instruments such as gift cards that can be used only with the issuing merchant or a group of merchants, cash withdrawals at ATMs, and certain securities and financial contract transactions. An agent of a registered PSP that is acting within its authority and is on the PSP's list of agents isn't separately covered.

Registration, review and fees

The Bank charges a one-time, non-refundable application fee. It was CA$2,500 when registration opened in November 2024, and it's set by regulation and adjusted for inflation each January. We confirm the current amount before you apply. The Bank plans to charge registered PSPs annual assessment fees to recover the cost of supervision, but it hasn't started yet. Its FAQ says it will begin charging them in due course, once the fee formula is finalized.

Application information is shared with the Department of Finance and with FINTRAC. If the Minister of Finance considers it necessary for national security reasons, the Minister can order a full review of an application, and the Bank can't register the applicant while that review is under way. Our RPAA registration service covers the application itself.

Ongoing obligations

Since September 8, 2025, PSPs must have a risk management and incident response framework, and those that hold end-user funds must safeguard them, either in a trust account or in a segregated account with insurance or a guarantee.

When a PSP becomes aware of an incident with a material impact, it must notify the Bank and the affected end users, PSPs or clearing houses without delay (RPAA section 18). The Bank's reporting reminder says the initial notice to the Bank is due no later than 48 hours after the PSP determines the incident is material. PSPs also give notice at least five business days before a significant change or new activity, and file an annual report by March 31. Our RPAA compliance program covers these frameworks.

RPAA and FINTRAC

The two regimes sit side by side. The RPAA deals with operational risk and safeguarding under the Bank of Canada. FINTRAC deals with money laundering, terrorist financing and sanctions evasion under the PCMLTFA. A payment business that also remits or transmits funds usually needs to register with FINTRAC as an MSB as well as with the Bank.

RPAA explained: common questions

Still not sure? Book a consultation or write to info@amlcanada.ca.

What does RPAA stand for?

The Retail Payment Activities Act, the law under which the Bank of Canada supervises payment service providers.

How much is the Bank of Canada's RPAA fee?

A one-time, non-refundable application fee. It was CA$2,500 when introduced in November 2024 and is adjusted for inflation each January, so we confirm the current amount before you apply. Annual assessment fees aren't charged yet.

Do foreign PSPs have to register under the RPAA?

Yes, if they direct retail payment activities at individuals or entities in Canada and perform them for end users in Canada.

When does a PSP have to notify the Bank of an incident?

Without delay, and no later than 48 hours after it determines that the incident has a material impact. Affected end users, PSPs and clearing houses must also be notified.

How long does RPAA registration take?

In our experience, generally about 2 to 8 months, including the national security review.
