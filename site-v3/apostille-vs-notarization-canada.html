Apostille vs notary in Canada: what each step does and who does it

Guide · Published 26 November 2026 · 6 min read · AML Canada

The apostille vs notary question comes down to who signs what. A notary public witnesses a signature, takes an oath or certifies a copy. An apostille is a certificate from a government competent authority that confirms that signature and seal, so the document can be used in another Apostille Convention country.

Key points

  • A notary performs an act on the document. An apostille certifies the signature, capacity and seal of the official or notary who signed it.
  • Global Affairs Canada and five provinces (Alberta, British Columbia, Ontario, Quebec and Saskatchewan) issue apostilles in Canada.
  • For a country outside the Apostille Convention, the document is authenticated in Canada and may then need legalization at that country's embassy or consulate.
  • Ontario won't apostille documents commissioned by a commissioner for taking affidavits or documents notarized remotely.

Apostille vs notary: what is the difference?

A notary public performs a notarial act on your document, such as witnessing a signature, receiving a declaration or affidavit, or certifying a true copy, according to the Global Affairs Canada glossary. An apostille comes later. It is a certificate from a government competent authority confirming the signature and seal already on the document.

The Apostille Convention sets out what the certificate covers. Under Article 5 of the Convention, an apostille certifies the authenticity of the signature, the capacity in which the signer acted and, where appropriate, the identity of the seal or stamp. Quebec says an apostille does not certify the document's content.

In Canada, notaries public are appointed by the province or territory where they practise, according to the glossary.

Apostille vs authentication vs legalization

Authentication, apostille and legalization are steps in the same chain. Global Affairs Canada's frequently asked questions say authentication proves that the signature and seal on a document are genuine, so another country can recognize it. An apostille is a specific type of authentication certificate, issued by parties to the 1961 Apostille Convention.

Legalization is a further certification by the destination country. Article 2 of the Convention defines it as the formality by which that country's diplomatic or consular agents certify the signature, the signer's capacity and any seal or stamp.

Article 3 makes the apostille the only formality a member country may require to certify a signature, capacity and seal. Canada acceded to the Convention on May 12, 2023, and it entered into force for Canada on January 11, 2024, according to the Hague Conference status table.

How can I apostille my documents? The Canadian chain

A Canadian document going abroad can pass through up to four hands: the receiving party, a notary, a competent authority and, for some countries, an embassy. The destination country's law decides whether you need authentication at all, according to Global Affairs Canada's requirements.

  1. The receiving party, or the destination country's nearest representative office, tells you what it needs; Global Affairs Canada says to check requirements with them.
  2. A notary public notarizes the document if needed. Global Affairs Canada expects a valid certificate of appointment, an ink signature, and a stamp or seal showing the notary's full name and province or territory.
  3. The competent authority issues the apostille or authentication certificate. Global Affairs Canada handles federal documents and documents issued or notarized in Manitoba, New Brunswick, Newfoundland and Labrador, Nova Scotia, Prince Edward Island and the three territories. A document notarized in Alberta, Ontario or Saskatchewan goes to that province. British Columbia and Quebec authenticate a notarized document only if the original was issued in the province.
  4. For a country outside the Convention, the destination's embassy, high commission or consulate may legalize the document.

When do you need a notary before the apostille?

You need a notary first when the competent authority can't verify the signature on the document itself. Global Affairs Canada says that if it can't authenticate a signature, for example because the signer isn't a public official known to its office, it may be able to authenticate a notarized copy.

Some government originals skip the notary. Ontario apostilles vital statistics documents, business registry documents issued since 1991, Superior Court of Justice and Ontario Court of Justice documents issued since July 10, 2023, and public postsecondary degrees, diplomas and transcripts issued since January 1, 2019, without notarization.

Plain photocopies can't be authenticated, but Global Affairs Canada may authenticate a copy certified by a notary. For Vital Statistics documents, it apostilles only originals or certified copies issued by those offices. Our guide to certified true copies explains who can certify a copy for each purpose.

In Quebec, a document issued by a person or body that isn't a public body but has an establishment in Quebec must be officially certified by a lawyer or notary, and the certificate must come with a signature authentication from the Barreau du Québec or the Chambre des notaires du Québec.

Can a commissioner of oaths do the notary's step?

A commissioner of oaths generally can't replace the notary for a document going for an apostille. The Ministry of the Attorney General says commissioned documents cannot be apostilled, and that Ontario's Official Documents Services doesn't process them.

In Ontario, a commissioner for taking affidavits may administer oaths and take affidavits, declarations and affirmations. A notary public has those powers too, and under section 3 of the Notaries Act can also attest the signing of a document and certify a true copy. Quebec says commissioners for oaths can't make copies or administer oaths for affidavits related to documents requiring an apostille.

Global Affairs Canada understands that commissioners for oaths in Canada generally aren't authorized to certify copies. Each province sets these powers, as our guide to commissioner of oaths vs notary public explains.

What countries accept apostilles?

Apostilles are accepted in countries where the Apostille Convention is in force with Canada. The Hague Conference status table listed 130 Contracting Parties at its last update on June 30, 2026, including some for which it isn't yet in force.

Under Article 12 of the Convention, an accession takes effect only between the acceding country and members that didn't object within six months, and the status table marks accessions that drew an objection. Global Affairs Canada also says some countries accept only its own authentication, so check with the destination before relying on a provincial apostille.

What is the fastest way to get an apostille?

The fastest route is a complete, eligible document sent to the right competent authority the first time, because Global Affairs Canada returns documents that don't meet its requirements unauthenticated. As of 26 November 2026, Global Affairs Canada was processing requests received 25 business days earlier, plus 5 to 10 business days of mailing time within Canada.

Global Affairs Canada offers no expedited service and considers priority processing only for urgent, exceptional circumstances outside your control. It says processing times are the same whether you apply directly or through a third-party service provider.

Apostille vs notary: common questions

Is a notarized document the same as an apostilled document?

No. A notarized document carries a notary's signature and seal. An apostilled document also carries a certificate from a competent authority confirming that signature and seal for use in an Apostille Convention country.

What is the purpose of an apostille?

It lets a public document from one Convention country be used in another without legalization. Under Article 5 of the Convention, it certifies the signature, the signer's capacity and any seal or stamp.

Do I need both a notary and an apostille?

Often. Private documents and copies are usually notarized first so the competent authority can verify a known signature. Some government originals, such as Ontario vital statistics documents, go straight to the authority.

Can a document notarized remotely in Ontario be apostilled?

No. The Notaries Act doesn't permit remote notarization, and Ontario's Official Documents Services won't authenticate or apostille documents notarized remotely.
