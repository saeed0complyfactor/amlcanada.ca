The RPAA compliance frameworks a PSP needs

Guide · Published 27 October 2026 · 7 min read · AML Canada

Every payment service provider needs a written risk management and incident response framework, and one that holds end-user funds also needs a safeguarding-of-funds framework. Third parties, agents and the senior officer's role are built into those two documents. Record keeping rules cover both, and the annual report, change notices and incident notices draw on them.

Key points

  • Every PSP needs a written risk management and incident response framework under section 17 of the RPAA and section 5 of the regulations.
  • A PSP that holds end-user funds also needs a safeguarding-of-funds framework under section 15 of the regulations.
  • Both frameworks are approved by the senior officer and any board at least once a year, and by the senior officer after each material change.
  • The framework can span several documents or adapt a parent company's, as long as it meets the RPAA, the regulations and the Bank's guideline.

The frameworks at a glance

The Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) and the Retail Payment Activities Regulations require two written frameworks. Every payment service provider (PSP) needs a risk management and incident response framework. A PSP that holds end-user funds also needs a safeguarding-of-funds framework. The Bank of Canada's supervisory framework says both requirements came into force on September 8, 2025.

The risk management and incident response framework

Section 17 of the Act requires this framework of every PSP, and the Bank can assess it and list corrective measures. Section 5 of the regulations says it must be in writing and must:

  • set objectives for keeping your payment activities available and preserving their integrity and confidentiality, with measurable reliability targets and indicators;
  • identify the human and financial resources needed, including skills and training;
  • allocate roles for normal operations and for incidents, with someone to challenge and oversee each role;
  • identify your assets and business processes and classify them by sensitivity and criticality;
  • identify your operational risks and their causes in eleven named areas, including business continuity and resilience, cybersecurity, fraud and third parties;
  • describe the controls for those risks and the continuous monitoring that detects incidents and lapses;
  • set out an incident response and recovery plan, including manual workarounds if primary systems fail, root cause investigation and a record of each incident.

All of it must be proportionate to the impact a breakdown would have on end users and other PSPs. The Bank's At a glance summary lists factors to tailor it to, such as your size, technology and risks. Sections 6 and 7 require you to keep it available to everyone with a role in it and to train them. Section 8 requires a review at least once a year and before any material change, with findings sent to the senior officer for approval. Section 9 requires a testing methodology that covers high-likelihood and high-impact risks, with a record of each test. If you have an internal or external auditor, section 10 adds an independent review at least every three years. Our incident reporting guide covers the notices.

Third-party service providers, agents and mandataries

Under subsection 5(3) of the regulations, you assess each third-party service provider that supports a payment function at least once a year, and before you enter into, renew, extend or substantially amend its contract. The assessment covers how it protects your data, the security of its connections to your systems, how it tells you about changes and breaches, how you monitor it, and its own risk management. Record the dates, scope and findings, and allocate responsibilities, including for data.

Subsection 5(4) requires criteria that agents and mandataries must meet, a ban on using any that don't, and a recorded assessment at least once a year. The Bank's operational risk guideline says an affiliate that provides payment-related services under a contract should be treated as a third-party service provider. Its FAQ adds that a bank can be one too. Section 87 of the Act makes you liable for violations committed by your third-party service providers, agents and mandataries within the scope of their role.

The safeguarding-of-funds framework

Only PSPs that hold end-user funds need this framework. Under section 15 of the regulations it must aim for reliable access to funds without delay, and for paying end users as soon as feasible if you become insolvent. It includes a ledger showing each end user's funds at the end of each day, how an insolvency administrator would get access to your records and contact end users, the procedures for returning funds, and an analysis of legal and operational risks. Unless the PSP is an individual, it names a senior officer responsible for safeguarding. Section 17 requires an independent review at least every three years. Our safeguarding guide covers trust accounts, insurance and guarantees.

Governance: the senior officer and the board

Unless the PSP is an individual, the risk framework must make a senior officer responsible for overseeing compliance with the framework and incident notice rules, and for overseeing material decisions on operational risks and incidents. The Bank's FAQ quotes the definition in section 1 of the regulations. A senior officer is:

  • a board member who is also a full-time employee;
  • the CEO, COO, CFO, president, chief risk officer or another officer the definition lists, or someone performing similar functions; or
  • any other officer who reports directly to the board, CEO or COO.

The senior officer doesn't have to be in Canada.

The senior officer receives review findings for approval, the record of each test, the gaps found by an independent review and information about incidents. The senior officer and the board, if you have one, approve each framework at least once a year, and the senior officer approves it again after each material change. The Bank's guideline says every significant change should count as material.

Record keeping

Section 40 of the regulations requires records sufficient to show that you comply with the Act and regulations, kept in a form the Bank can understand. You keep them until five years after the day they stop showing current compliance. Section 41 requires reasonable measures against loss, falsification and unauthorized access, and section 42 extends both rules to records held by your agents and third-party service providers, which must stay accessible to you.

Reports and notices tied to the frameworks

Three filings rely on the frameworks:

  • The annual report, required by section 21 of the Act and due by March 31 for the previous calendar year (regulations section 18). It asks about your risk framework and, if you hold funds, your safeguarding.
  • A significant change or new activity notice, at least five business days ahead (regulations section 20). A change is significant if it could reasonably be expected to have a material impact on operational risks or safeguarding. The notice lists the framework documents you changed and, if you have senior officers, confirms one approved the change. The Bank's significant change guideline gives examples such as switching from a trust account to insurance. Its paragraph 3.3 adds changes that may qualify depending on your circumstances, such as entering, amending or ending a third-party service provider agreement, or switching cloud providers.
  • Incident notices. Section 18 of the Act requires you to notify the Bank and each materially affected end user, PSP or designated clearing house without delay. The Bank's incident notification guideline sets the outer limit at 48 hours after you determine the incident is material.

Changes to your list of agents, or to third-party service providers that materially affect your operational risks or safeguarding, are also reported to the Bank within 30 days under section 59 of the Act and section 35 of the regulations.

Gaps to check

The Bank's FAQ allows a framework spread over several documents, or an existing or parent-company framework that meets the requirements. Before the Bank asks for your policies in a desk or on-site assessment, check for these gaps:

  • Objectives with no measurable reliability targets or indicators.
  • Reviews held once a year but not before material changes.
  • Tests with no record of methodology, results or follow-up.
  • Third parties assessed only at onboarding, or affiliates and banks left off the list.
  • No written criteria for agents, or no annual agent assessment.
  • A senior officer who doesn't meet the definition, or approvals with no record.
  • A safeguarding framework not reviewed after an account or insurer change.

The schedule to the regulations classifies a breach of section 17(1) of the Act, read with section 5 of the regulations, as a very serious violation. Paragraph 48(1)(b) of the regulations sets the penalty for a very serious violation at up to $10,000,000. RPAA compliance frameworks: common questions

Still not sure? Book a consultation or write to info@amlcanada.ca.

Does every PSP need a risk management and incident response framework?

Yes. Section 17 of the RPAA requires every payment service provider that performs retail payment activities to have one. Only the safeguarding-of-funds framework is limited to PSPs that hold end-user funds.

Does the framework have to be one document?

No. The Bank's FAQ says it need not be a single document, provided it meets the guideline. The Bank doesn't plan to publish an example framework.

Can a PSP use its parent company's framework?

Yes, if it meets the RPAA, the regulations and the Bank's guideline. If adopting it leaves gaps, the guideline says the PSP must change the framework or add supplementary arrangements.

Who approves the RPAA frameworks?

The senior officer and the board of directors, if there is one, at least once a year. The senior officer also approves each framework after every material change.

Is an independent review of the risk framework always required?

Only for PSPs that have an internal or external auditor, at least every three years. A PSP that holds end-user funds needs an independent review of safeguarding compliance at least every three years regardless.

RPAA safeguarding of end-user funds

Since September 8, 2025, a payment service provider that holds funds for its users must safeguard them, either in trust in a trust account or in a segregated account backed by insurance or a guarantee. It also needs a written safeguarding framework and an independent review at least every three years.

RPAA incident reporting and the 48-hour rule

When a payment service provider becomes aware of an incident with a material impact, it must notify the Bank of Canada and the affected end users, payment service providers and clearing houses without delay. The Bank expects the first notice no later than 48 hours after the provider decides the incident is material.

The RPAA annual report for payment service providers

Every payment service provider that performs retail payment activities in a calendar year must file an annual report with the Bank of Canada by March 31 of the following year. It covers your risk management and incident response framework, safeguarding, activity metrics and financial results.
