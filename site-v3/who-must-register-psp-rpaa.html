Who must register as a PSP under the RPAA?

Guide · Published 21 October 2026 · 6 min read · AML Canada

You must register with the Bank of Canada if you perform a payment function as a business in its own right, for electronic transfers in Canadian or foreign currency, with a link to Canada, and no exclusion applies. This guide shows how the Bank applies each part of that test to real business models.

Key points

  • Register if you perform a payment function as a distinct business, for fiat transfers, with a link to Canada, and no exclusion applies.
  • You can be a PSP without holding funds. Storing a customer's details for future transfers can be enough.
  • Payments are incidental only when they exist to support another business. Separate fees, user expectations and payment marketing point the other way.
  • Agents of a registered PSP are covered by it. Third-party providers and agents of excluded banks are not.

The four-part test

The Bank of Canada's supervisory policy, Criteria for registering payment service providers (last updated June 29, 2026), sets out a four-part test. You must register if all four parts are true:

  1. You perform at least one of the five payment functions as a service or business activity that isn't incidental to another service or business activity. That makes you a payment service provider (PSP).
  2. The payment function relates to an electronic funds transfer (EFT) in Canadian dollars, a foreign currency or a unit that meets prescribed criteria. That makes it a retail payment activity. Cash isn't an EFT.
  3. You have a place of business in Canada, or you direct retail payment activities at people or entities in Canada and perform them for end users here.
  4. Neither you nor the activity is excluded.

Registration must be in place before you perform any retail payment activities (RPAA section 23). The Bank's FAQ says every PSP in scope must register for itself, regardless of size or revenue.

How the Bank reads the five payment functions

Section 2 of the Act lists the payment functions. The Bank's policy explains how it reads each one, and some reach further than businesses expect:

  • Providing or maintaining an account. Storing personal or financial information about an identifiable end user for future EFTs counts. You don't need to hold any funds. Collecting data for a single transaction usually doesn't count.
  • Holding funds. Funds are held when they're at rest, available for later withdrawal or transfer, and owed to the end user. Funds in transit aren't held. If you do hold funds, our safeguarding guide explains what follows.
  • Initiating an EFT. You initiate a transfer when you create the first payment instruction by capturing and packaging the payer's or payee's data. This covers push and pull payments.
  • Authorizing or transmitting instructions. This includes enabling consent, confirming funds are available, and providing the platforms or interfaces that carry payment instructions.
  • Clearing or settlement. Clearing includes netting and calculating positions before settlement. Settlement includes posting debits and credits. Software used only for a merchant's accounting or reporting usually doesn't count.

An end user is the payer or payee, who may not be your direct customer when several PSPs are involved.

The incidental test

A business that performs payment functions only to support another business isn't a PSP. The Act builds this into the PSP definition, and section 3 of the Retail Payment Activities Regulations excludes an incidental retail payment activity unless the other business itself consists of performing a payment function.

The Bank's policy says payment functions may be incidental if they exist only to support your non-payment services or improve the customer's experience of them. You're more likely to be a PSP if payments are a distinct business, especially when you facilitate payments between other buyers and sellers. The Bank weighs three indicators:

  • revenue or commercial advantage, including indirect benefits such as data that could later be monetized;
  • whether end users reasonably expect to receive a payment service;
  • marketing and branding that promote payments.

The Bank's merchant scenarios show where the line falls. An online boot retailer that stores a customer's name and address to speed up checkout maintains an account, but the function is incidental and it doesn't register. If the same retailer lets unaffiliated merchants accept payments through its app for a fee on each transaction, payments become a business line of their own and the retailer must register. The Bank expects businesses to reassess as they change.

Place of business and foreign PSPs

The Bank treats you as having a place of business in Canada if you have a physical location here, including a home office, are incorporated here, or have employees, agents or mandataries here.

A PSP with no place of business in Canada is still covered if it performs retail payment activities for end users in Canada and directs those activities at Canada (RPAA section 5). To locate end users, the Bank looks at factors such as IP address, shipping or service address and whether the product is localized for Canada. Signs of directing activities at Canada include marketing aimed at Canadians, a .ca domain, offering Canadian dollars or Canadian support, and a target market largely in Canada.

Marketing only to test demand doesn't trigger registration, but serving end users here does. In the Bank's geographic scope scenarios, a foreign payment app that reaches Canadian merchants through an agreement with a Canadian PSP must register, and a foreign app's Canadian subsidiary registers in its own right. A foreign applicant also names an agent or mandatary in Canada authorized to accept notices and orders for it (RPAA paragraph 29(1)(o)).

Exclusions: the details that decide close cases

Our RPAA overview lists the exclusions in sections 6 to 10 of the Act. These points from the Bank's policy settle most close cases:

  • Affiliates of excluded institutions. Banks, credit unions, insurers and other institutions listed in section 9 are outside the Act. Their affiliates must register unless OSFI or the provincial regulator supervises them on a consolidated basis.
  • Merchant instruments. Instruments usable only at the issuing merchant or within a specific group of merchants are excluded. Wide-use instruments may not be.
  • Securities transactions. Payments that give effect to securities transactions are excluded only when performed by an entity regulated, or exempted from regulation, under Canadian securities law. Its other payments may still be in scope.
  • Designated systems. Payment functions performed using a system designated under the Payment Clearing and Settlement Act are excluded (section 7). Functions you perform outside the system are not.
  • Internal transactions. Transfers between affiliated entities are excluded only when no other PSP is involved (section 8). Payments to unaffiliated customers don't qualify, even if they move through an internal ledger.

Agents, mandataries and third-party providers

Section 10 takes an agent or mandatary of a registered PSP outside the Act while it acts within its authority and appears on the list of agents the PSP gave the Bank. The PSP keeps that list up to date and is liable for violations committed by its employees, agents, mandataries and third-party service providers within the scope of their role (section 87).

The Bank's agent scenarios draw three lines. A retailer that pays out cash only on behalf of a registered remittance company doesn't register. An agent that also offers payment services to its own customers must register for those services. An agent acting for a bank, which is excluded rather than registered, may have to register itself.

Third-party service providers, which supply a PSP with a payment-related service under contract, aren't excluded. The PSP discloses them and stays responsible for them. Each affiliated PSP in a group also registers separately, even when its parent is registered.

Before you apply

Run the four parts for each product line and keep a written record. The Bank's refusal policy lists, among its grounds, that the RPAA doesn't apply to the applicant. Many businesses that register with the Bank also need FINTRAC registration. Who must register as a PSP: common questions

Still not sure? Book a consultation or write to info@amlcanada.ca.

Do I need to register with the Bank of Canada if I don't hold customer funds?

Possibly. Holding funds is only one of the five payment functions. A payment gateway that captures card data and relays it to a processor is a PSP in the Bank's own example, even though it never handles funds.

Do crypto exchanges need to register under the RPAA?

It depends on the model. In the Bank's examples, an exchange dealing only in cryptocurrency doesn't register, and neither does one whose fiat functions are incidental to trading and covered by the securities exclusions. A crypto-backed prepaid card that earns interchange fees is a PSP.

Does an online marketplace need to register as a PSP?

Under the Bank's December 2025 policy, a marketplace that receives sale proceeds in an account it owns or controls is a PSP, even if it uses another PSP for part of the process. A marketplace whose funds never pass through its own account generally isn't.

Do foreign payment companies have to register with the Bank of Canada?

Yes, if they perform retail payment activities for end users in Canada and direct those activities at Canada. Marketing only to test demand doesn't trigger registration, but serving Canadian end users does.

Is there a minimum size or revenue for RPAA registration?

No. The Bank's FAQ says every PSP in scope must register for itself, regardless of size and whether or not the business generates revenue.

What is the RPAA?

The Retail Payment Activities Act is the federal law under which the Bank of Canada supervises payment service providers. It sets rules on operational risk, incidents and the safeguarding of users' funds.

Read the guide RPAA

RPAA vs FINTRAC MSB registration: do you need both?

Payment service providers register with the Bank of Canada under the RPAA. Money services businesses register with FINTRAC under the PCMLTFA. Many payment businesses are both, and the two registrations are linked: the Bank shares your application with FINTRAC and can refuse it based on what FINTRAC reports.

Read the guide RPAA

RPAA safeguarding of end-user funds

Since September 8, 2025, a payment service provider that holds funds for its users must safeguard them, either in trust in a trust account or in a segregated account backed by insurance or a guarantee. It also needs a written safeguarding framework and an independent review at least every three years.
