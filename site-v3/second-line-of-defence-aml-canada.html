The second line of defence in AML: how the three lines map to FINTRAC rules

Guide · Published 19 November 2026 · 6 min read · AML Canada

The second line of defence in AML is the compliance function that sets the rules, checks the operations team's work and decides on the files the team escalates. The term comes from the three lines of defence, a widely used governance model. FINTRAC's compliance program guidance and assessment manual don't use it, so this guide maps each line onto what the law requires of an MSB.

Key points

  • "Lines of defence" is an industry model. As of 19 November 2026, FINTRAC's compliance program guidance and examination manual don't use the term.
  • The law puts every duty on the business, and PCMLTFR s. 156 lists the program elements.
  • The second line is that compliance officer: they write the rules, review what operations escalates and decide whether a transaction is reported.
  • The two-year effectiveness review is done by an auditor, or by the business if it has none. FINTRAC's best practice is a reviewer not directly involved in the program.

What is the second line of defence in AML?

In the three lines of defence model, the second line is the compliance function. It sets the anti-money laundering rules the business follows, monitors whether the operations team applies them, and challenges or decides on cases the team can't settle alone. In a Canadian money services business, that role sits with the compliance officer.

The model is a way of dividing work. It isn't a legal category in Canada. As of 19 November 2026, FINTRAC's compliance program guidance, its assessment manual and its risk assessment guidance don't mention lines of defence. What the law does require is a compliance program, and the model is one way to staff it.

What are the first, second and third lines of defence?

The first line is the business itself: the people who onboard clients and process transactions, and who own the risk those activities create. The second line is the risk and compliance function that sets standards and oversees the first. The third line is internal audit or a similar function, which gives independent assurance that the other two work.

Banks will recognize the pattern from OSFI. Its Guideline E-21 on operational risk management and resilience (August 2024) applies to federally regulated financial institutions, so here it is context and not an expectation for MSBs. It describes the same three layers without the label: business and central functions accountable for managing operational risks, independent risk and compliance functions that oversee and challenge them, and internal audit or a similar function that provides independent assurance to senior management and the board.

How the three lines map onto FINTRAC rules

The PCMLTFA gives every duty to the business. Section 6 requires it to keep records, section 6.1 to verify identity, section 7 to report suspicious transactions, and section 9.6 to run a compliance program that is "reasonably designed, risk-based and effective". The lines of defence decide which people carry out each duty. This table maps the model onto those provisions for an MSB.

Who owns the first line in an MSB?

The first line is everyone who deals with clients or handles their funds. Section 133 of the regulations says that when an employee does the work, the employer is responsible for complying with the regulations. When an agent acts for the business, the business is responsible. Staff carry out the controls, but the legal duty stays with the MSB.

FINTRAC's guidance says training should reach people who have contact with clients, such as front-line staff or agents, and people who handle cash, funds or virtual currency. Its assessment manual says examiners may interview employees and agents to confirm they know the requirements that apply to their duties.

What a second line compliance review covers

A second line compliance review is the compliance officer's check of first-line work. It covers the onboarding files, transactions and monitoring alerts the team escalates, and each review should end in a recorded decision. Paragraph 156(1)(a) of the regulations makes the appointed person responsible for implementing the program, and that review work is part of implementing it.

In practice, the second line:

  • writes and updates the policies, procedures and risk assessment that tell the first line what to do (s. 156(1)(b) and (c));
  • trains staff on their part of the program (s. 156(1)(d) and (e));
  • reviews escalated clients and decides to approve, reject or ask for more information;
  • reviews alerts the first line can't clear, against the ongoing monitoring purposes in section 123.1, such as checking whether activity is consistent with what the business knows about the client;
  • decides whether there are reasonable grounds to suspect, which triggers a suspicious transaction report under PCMLTFA s. 7.

FINTRAC's guidance lets the compliance officer delegate duties to other employees. The officer still remains responsible for implementing the program, and the business remains responsible for meeting its requirements. Who can hold the role, and what they need to know, is covered in our guide to FINTRAC compliance officer requirements.

How a small business keeps the lines separate

A small MSB can't put a different team on each line, and the law doesn't ask it to. FINTRAC's guidance says the owner or operator of a small business can be the compliance officer. Its best practice is that the compliance officer of a larger business shouldn't be directly involved in the receipt, transfer or payment of funds.

FINTRAC's guidance also says the compliance officer should have independent oversight and be able to communicate directly with senior management or the board. With few staff, separation comes from process. In our view, these steps keep the lines apart:

  • Write down which files the first line must escalate, and what each escalated file must contain.
  • Have the person who onboarded a client avoid approving that same client when it is escalated.
  • Log every second-line decision with its reasons, including decisions not to file a report.
  • Bring in someone outside daily operations for the second line, the third line, or both.

How an outside compliance officer acts as the second line

An outsourced compliance officer sits on top of your operations team. Your team does the first-line work and escalates, and the officer reviews and decides. In our engagements, all onboarding and transaction escalations go through one shared channel. A trained point of contact in your operations team posts each file with the information we need.

Each escalation follows four steps:

  1. Your point of contact escalates the client file or transaction in the shared channel.
  2. We review it and run a customer risk assessment under your compliance framework.
  3. We decide to approve, reject or request more information.
  4. Your operations or support team applies the decision and sends any request to the customer.

Monitoring is shared. We help deploy monitoring rules and thresholds and review the alerts and transactions your team escalates. Your team runs day-to-day monitoring, turns rules on and off, and releases transactions that don't need our review. Building and integrating systems stays with your team or its providers. Our role is advisory: we make sure documents and processes meet the requirements, and applying them in practice is your responsibility.

The day-to-day workflow is set out in how an outsourced compliance officer works, and scope and fee are on our outsourced compliance officer page.

Why the second line shouldn't review itself

The third line tests whether the first two work. FINTRAC's best practice, to keep the review impartial, is a reviewer who isn't directly involved in your compliance program activities. Under PCMLTFR s. 156(3), the review is carried out and documented every two years by an internal or external auditor, or by the business itself if it has no auditor. The Regulations name who may do it, and FINTRAC's guidance adds the impartiality point.

FINTRAC's guidance says the reviewer should know the requirements under the Act and regulations. As a best practice, to keep the review impartial, it shouldn't be done by someone directly involved in your compliance program activities. For an entity, the findings, policy updates and implementation status go in writing to a senior officer within 30 days after the review is completed (s. 156(4)).

For that reason, when we act as compliance officer we prepare the documents and records for the two-year review and don't conduct it, and you appoint the independent reviewer. What the review tests is in our effectiveness review checklist, and our effectiveness reviews page covers having one done.

Second line of defence: common questions

What is the 2nd line of defence in risk?

It is the risk and compliance function in the three lines of defence model. It sets the rules, oversees day-to-day work and decides on escalated cases. In a Canadian MSB, that is the compliance officer appointed under PCMLTFR s. 156(1)(a).

Does FINTRAC require the three lines of defence model?

No. As of 19 November 2026, FINTRAC's compliance program guidance and examination manual don't use the term. The law requires a compliance program with the elements in PCMLTFR s. 156, and the three lines are one way to staff it.

Can the compliance officer do the two-year effectiveness review?

The law says an internal or external auditor does the review, or the business itself if it has no auditor (PCMLTFR s. 156(3)). FINTRAC's best practice is that the reviewer shouldn't be directly involved in the compliance program activities, which in most cases rules out the compliance officer.

Can a small MSB with two or three staff have three lines of defence?

Yes, through process. FINTRAC's guidance lets the owner or operator of a small business be the compliance officer. Written escalation rules, recorded decisions and an outside reviewer keep the work separate.

Is an outsourced compliance officer the second line?

Yes, when they are appointed as the person responsible for implementing the program. FINTRAC's guidance says the officer stays responsible for implementing it, and the business stays responsible for meeting its requirements.
