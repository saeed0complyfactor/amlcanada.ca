Certified true copy in Canada: who can certify and when you need a notary

Guide · Published 26 November 2026 · 6 min read · AML Canada

A certified true copy is a photocopy that an authorized person has compared with the original and marked as a true copy. Canada has no single list of who may certify one. IRCC, Service Canada and Public Safety Canada each publish their own list, the Passport Program certifies passport copies itself, and Global Affairs Canada accepts notary-certified copies for authentication. In practice, a copy going abroad with an apostille usually needs a notary.

Key points

  • Several Canadian authorities publish their own list of who may certify a copy, so check the recipient's rule first.
  • IRCC, Service Canada and Public Safety Canada accept a commissioner of oaths, but Global Affairs Canada says commissioners generally aren't authorized to certify copies for authentication.
  • A copy for use abroad with an apostille usually needs a notary, and Ontario won't apostille commissioned or remotely notarized documents.
  • Some recipients prefer a copy certified by the office that issued the original, such as Corporations Canada at $40 per document.

What is a certified true copy?

A certified true copy is a photocopy of an original document that an authorized person has compared with the original and certified as a true copy. IRCC's glossary says the person marks the photocopy with their name and signature, their position or title, the name of the original document, the date, and the statement "I certify that this is a true copy of the original document."

Some bodies use the term more narrowly. Quebec's health insurance board, RAMQ, defines a certified true copy as a copy whose official value is recognized by the organization that issued the original, and says it isn't a simple photocopy. Before you start, ask the recipient which of the two it means.

Who can certify a true copy in Canada?

Who can certify a true copy in Canada depends on who asked for it: several authorities publish their own list. A commissioner of oaths is on the IRCC, Service Canada and Public Safety Canada lists, but not the Global Affairs Canada one. As of 26 November 2026, the official pages say:

A foreign bank, university or regulator sets its own rule, and none of these Canadian lists binds it. Ask the recipient in writing who it accepts as a certifier and whether it also needs an apostille.

How do you get a certified true copy?

To get a certified true copy, take the original and a clear photocopy to a person the recipient accepts. That person compares the two and certifies the copy. Service Canada's instructions (form ISP-1730) ask the certifier to:

  • compare the original document with the photocopy;
  • state their official position or title, and sign and print their name;
  • give their telephone number;
  • write the date they certified the document;
  • write "This photocopy is a true copy of the original document which has not been altered in any way."

For a document of more than one page, Service Canada says to copy every page. The certifier can certify each page, or only the first page if they state the total number of pages, including blank ones. Service Canada refuses a copy that is missing any of these elements.

IRCC asks for different wording, so use the statement of the authority you're sending the copy to. For a translated document, IRCC's translation instructions say the translator must stamp both the certified copy and the translation.

Copies certified by the issuing office

Many offices certify copies of their own records, and some recipients prefer that. The Quebec immigration ministry says a copy certified by the document issuer is always the preferred format. As of 26 November 2026:

  • Corporations Canada charges $40 per document for certified copies of corporate documents, requested by email or mail only, and notes that some institutions, such as courts, may require them.
  • Ontario family courts charge $1 per page for hard copies or $3.50 to $4.00 for each page certified by court staff, requested from the court location where the case started.
  • The Passport Program certifies copies of Canadian passports. A copy with an ink stamp uses form PPTC 670 and needs a clear and strong reason.

Global Affairs Canada applies the same idea to vital statistics records. It issues apostilles only for originals or for certified copies issued by those offices, and not for copies certified by a notary public.

Notarized copy vs certified true copy vs apostilled copy

A notarized copy is a certified true copy made by a notary public. Under section 3 of Ontario's Notaries Act, a notary may certify and attest a true copy of a document, according to the Ministry of the Attorney General. An apostilled copy adds a further step: a competent authority confirms the notary's signature and seal so the copy can be used in another Apostille Convention country.

Certified true copyA person on the requesting authority's list, or the issuing officeThe copy matches the originalCanadian applications such as IRCC, CPP and OAS

Global Affairs Canada's FAQ says authentication proves that the signature and seal on a document are genuine. Our guide to apostille vs notarization walks through the full chain, including legalization for countries outside the Convention.

When does a copy need a notary and an apostille?

A copy going to a foreign authority usually needs a notary's certification and then an apostille, if the destination country asks for one. Global Affairs Canada says it can't authenticate plain photocopies without an original signature and seal, and may authenticate copies certified by a notary.

Ontario's rules are stricter. The Ministry of the Attorney General says commissioned documents can't be apostilled and Ontario's Official Documents Services doesn't process them. An Ontario notary must be physically present, and documents notarized remotely won't be authenticated or apostilled. As of 26 November 2026, Ontario charges $16 to authenticate a notarized legal document.

Global Affairs Canada says a certified, notarized copy can be sent without the original, and suggests one if you want to keep an original such as a diploma unaltered and the destination country accepts copies. Our guides to getting an apostille in Canada and apostille in Ontario cover which authority to send it to.

How much does a certified true copy cost?

The cost of a certified true copy depends on who makes it. As of 26 November 2026, Service Canada staff certify documents for CPP and OAS applications free of charge at any Service Canada Centre. Corporations Canada charges $40 per certified document, and Ontario family courts charge $3.50 to $4.00 per certified page.

The official pages we checked don't set fees for notaries or lawyers, so ask for a quote. For a passport copy, check the Passport Program's fee list.

Certified copies of corporate documents

For corporate records going abroad, such as articles or resolutions, the receiving bank or regulator decides whether a Corporations Canada certified copy will do or a notarized and apostilled copy is needed. Our guide to apostilling corporate documents lists which records need notarizing, and our comparison of a commissioner of oaths vs a notary public explains what each may sign.

Our apostille and notarization service coordinates this for corporate documents only, with notarization by a licensed Ontario partner notary or lawyer. AML Canada isn't a notary and doesn't handle personal documents such as birth certificates, degrees or police checks.

What is meant by certified true copy?

A photocopy that an authorized person has compared with the original and certified as a true copy, with their name, signature, title and the date. Some bodies, such as RAMQ, use the term for a copy certified by the office that issued the original.

Who can certify a true copy in Canada?

It depends on who asks. IRCC gives notaries public and commissioners as examples, Service Canada accepts its own staff and a list of professionals, and Global Affairs Canada may authenticate copies certified by a notary.

Can a commissioner of oaths certify a true copy?

For IRCC, Service Canada and Public Safety Canada, yes. Global Affairs Canada says commissioners generally aren't authorized to certify copies, and Ontario won't apostille commissioned documents.

Is a notarized copy the same as a certified true copy?

A notarized copy is a certified true copy made by a notary public. A copy certified by someone else, such as a commissioner of oaths, may satisfy a Canadian agency, but Ontario says it won't apostille commissioned documents, so it may not be accepted there.
