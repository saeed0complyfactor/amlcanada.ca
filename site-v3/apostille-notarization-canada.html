What an apostille is

Authentication confirms that the signature and seal of a public official on a document are genuine, so the document can be recognized in another country. An apostille is a type of authentication certificate accepted by every country that is a member of the Apostille Convention, according to the Global Affairs Canada glossary. Global Affairs Canada issues it as a separate page that it securely attaches to your document.

An apostille confirms where a document came from. It doesn't confirm what the document says. Ontario's Official Documents Services checks signatures and seals against its records and doesn't validate the contents.

Canada and the Apostille Convention

Canada acceded to the Hague Apostille Convention on May 12, 2023, and the Convention entered into force for Canada on January 11, 2024, according to the Hague Conference status table. The table listed 130 contracting parties when we checked it. For documents going to a member country, the apostille replaces legalization at that country's embassy or consulate.

Whether a document needs authentication at all is set by the law of the destination country. Canadian law doesn't require it, so start with the receiving bank, partner or regulator.

Who issues the apostille

Canada has six authorities that issue apostilles. The Global Affairs Canada requirements set out who handles what:

  • Global Affairs Canada: documents issued by the Government of Canada, such as Corporations Canada documents, and documents issued or notarized in Manitoba, New Brunswick, Newfoundland and Labrador, the Northwest Territories, Nova Scotia, Nunavut, Prince Edward Island or Yukon.
  • Alberta, British Columbia, Ontario, Quebec and Saskatchewan: documents issued in their own province.

A document notarized in Alberta, Ontario or Saskatchewan goes to that province, wherever it was originally issued. British Columbia and Quebec authenticate a notarized document only if the original was issued in their province. In practice, a copy of a federal certificate notarized by an Ontario notary goes to Ontario's Official Documents Services, not to Global Affairs Canada.

When a document must be notarized first

Some government documents can be apostilled directly. Most private and corporate records can't. The Global Affairs Canada document eligibility tool says:

  • a Corporations Canada certificate of incorporation with an original signature or seal doesn't need notarizing, but one without an original signature or seal does;
  • certificates of compliance and existence, articles of incorporation, business registration documents, corporate bylaws and other business or corporate records must be notarized;
  • powers of attorney, as private legal documents, must be notarized.

Ontario's Official Documents Services accepts business registry documents issued since 1991 by the Business and Personal Property Branch or the Ontario Business Registry without notarization. It won't accept documents commissioned by a commissioner for taking affidavits, or electronic copies.

In Ontario, a notary must be physically present with the person when notarizing. The Ministry of the Attorney General says remote notarization isn't permitted under the Notaries Act, and Official Documents Services won't authenticate or apostille documents notarized remotely. Our partner notary works in person.

Countries that are not part of the Convention

For a country outside the Convention, the document may need to be legalized at that country's embassy, high commission or consulate after it has been authenticated in Canada, according to Global Affairs Canada. Ontario issues a certificate of authentication instead of an apostille for those countries. Global Affairs Canada also notes that some countries accept only its own authentication, so check with the destination country's mission before choosing a route.

How we handle your documents

  1. Consultation. Which documents you need, which country they're going to and what the receiving party has asked for.
  2. Document check. We confirm whether each document can be apostilled directly or needs notarization first, and which authority issues it. If you need a fresh certificate of status or certified copies, we tell you what to order.
  3. Notarization. Our licensed Ontario partner notary notarizes the documents that need it, in person.
  4. Apostille submission. We submit each document to the correct competent authority with the government fee.
  5. Return. Once the apostilles are issued, we courier the documents back to you.

Apostille & notarization: questions

What is an apostille?

A certificate that confirms the signature and seal of the official on a Canadian public document are genuine, so the document is accepted in other countries that belong to the Apostille Convention. It doesn't confirm the document's contents.

When did Canada join the Apostille Convention?

Canada acceded on May 12, 2023, and the Convention entered into force for Canada on January 11, 2024.

Can a Corporations Canada certificate be apostilled without notarization?

A certificate of incorporation with an original signature or seal can go to Global Affairs Canada directly. Global Affairs Canada says other business and corporate records, including certificates of compliance and existence and articles, must be notarized.

Is AML Canada a notary?

No. We coordinate the process. Notarization is done by a licensed notary public in Ontario who we work with.

Can documents be notarized remotely?

No. Ontario's Notaries Act doesn't permit remote notarization, and Ontario won't apostille a document notarized remotely.

How much does an apostille cost?

Our fee is quoted per document set. Government fees are separate: Global Affairs Canada charges nothing, and Ontario charges CA$16 per notarized document and CA$32 per Ontario government document.
