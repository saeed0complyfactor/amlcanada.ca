How to get an apostille in Canada (2026 guide)

Guide · Published 21 October 2026 · 5 min read · AML Canada

Since January 11, 2024, Canadian public documents going to Apostille Convention countries get an apostille. Who issues it depends on where the document was issued or notarized: Global Affairs Canada, or one of five provinces.

Key points

  • The Apostille Convention has applied in Canada since January 11, 2024.
  • Alberta, British Columbia, Ontario, Quebec and Saskatchewan apostille their own documents. Global Affairs Canada handles federal documents and the rest of Canada.
  • A document notarized in Alberta, Ontario or Saskatchewan goes to that province, wherever it was issued.
  • Global Affairs Canada charges no fee. Provincial fees run from CA$16 to CA$66.50 per document.

What an apostille does

An apostille is a certificate that confirms the signature and seal of a public official on a document are genuine. Countries that belong to the Apostille Convention accept it without any further step, according to the Global Affairs Canada glossary. Before the Convention, a Canadian document usually had to be authenticated in Canada and then legalized by the destination country's embassy or consulate. The apostille replaces that chain with a single certificate.

Canada acceded to the Convention on May 12, 2023, and it entered into force for Canada on January 11, 2024, according to the Hague Conference status table. An apostille doesn't validate what a document says. It confirms where the document came from.

Step 1: Confirm what the destination needs

Whether you need an apostille is decided by the law of the country where the document will be used. Canadian law doesn't require it. Global Affairs Canada and Ontario's Official Documents Services both tell applicants to check with the receiving institution or the destination country's embassy or consulate first. Neither office can advise on what a foreign authority wants.

Ask how recent the document must be, whether a certified copy is acceptable and whether any translation also needs an apostille. Quebec advises applicants to make sure the issue date meets the requesting entity's requirements and to use recent documents where possible.

Step 2: Find the right authority

Canada has six competent authorities: the federal government and five provinces. The Global Affairs Canada requirements split the work like this:

Where the document comes fromWho issues the apostille
Government of Canada (federal documents)Global Affairs Canada
Manitoba, New Brunswick, Newfoundland and Labrador, Northwest Territories, Nova Scotia, Nunavut, Prince Edward Island, YukonGlobal Affairs Canada, for documents issued or notarized there
Alberta, Ontario, SaskatchewanThe province, for documents issued there and for any document notarized there

Global Affairs Canada's online eligibility tool tells you where to send a given document. Quebec says it doesn't apostille documents issued by a federal authority, a territory or another province. Global Affairs Canada also notes that some countries accept only its authentication, so confirm the destination accepts a provincial apostille.

Step 3: Notarize the document if needed

Government-issued originals with an original signature or seal can often be apostilled directly. Private documents, and many business records, must be notarized first. Plain photocopies can't be authenticated, but Global Affairs Canada may accept a copy certified by a notary. It also says commissioners for oaths generally aren't authorized to certify copies.

When a notary signs a document for authentication, Global Affairs Canada expects the notary to:

  • have a valid certificate of appointment;
  • sign by hand in ink;
  • apply a stamp or seal showing their full name and the province or territory of appointment;
  • state the type of notarial act and the date;
  • show when their appointment expires, if it does.

Provincial rules add to this. In Ontario the notary must be physically present, and the Ministry of the Attorney General says documents notarized remotely won't be authenticated or apostilled. Ontario also needs the notary's full signature and seal on the same page. Alberta asks for corporate records and powers of attorney to be notarized by an Alberta notary. Quebec requires an official certificate from a lawyer or notary for private documents, with a signature authentication from the Barreau du Québec or the Chambre des notaires du Québec.

Step 4: Arrange translations

Global Affairs Canada needs a certified translation for any content not in English or French, sent with the original. The translator must belong to a recognized provincial translation association, or a Canadian notary who speaks both languages must attest the translation. Ontario adds that a document in another language must be issued in Canada, notarized by an Ontario notary and accompanied by a translation.

Step 5: Submit, pay and wait

Global Affairs Canada takes requests through its online portal. You then mail or courier the documents, or drop them at its distribution centre in Ottawa, which offers no in-person service. Include a prepaid return envelope or label if you want tracking. Ontario accepts mail-in requests at its Toronto office and in-person requests in Toronto, Ottawa, Sault Ste. Marie, Windsor and Thunder Bay. Alberta and British Columbia take documents by mail or courier, and Quebec by mail.

After you receive the apostille

Apostilles from Global Affairs Canada don't expire. Before January 11, 2024, Global Affairs Canada authenticated documents with a red stamp, and a red-stamped document can't be used in a Convention country. You need a new request with a clean version of the document. Global Affairs Canada's verification tool lets a recipient check apostilles it issued and those issued by Alberta, Saskatchewan and British Columbia. Ontario runs its own validity check.

Countries outside the Convention

For a country that hasn't joined the Convention, the document may need legalization at that country's embassy, high commission or consulate after it's authenticated in Canada. Ontario issues a certificate of authentication for those countries. Alberta and Quebec issue an apostille either way, and both say legalization may still be needed. Global Affairs Canada can forward authenticated documents to an embassy or high commission in Ottawa on request.

Apostille in Canada: common questions

Still not sure? Book a consultation or write to info@amlcanada.ca.

Who issues apostilles in Canada?

Global Affairs Canada issues them for federal documents and for documents from Manitoba, New Brunswick, Newfoundland and Labrador, Nova Scotia, Prince Edward Island and the three territories. Alberta, British Columbia, Ontario, Quebec and Saskatchewan issue them for their own documents.

How much does an apostille cost in Canada?

Global Affairs Canada charges no fee. Ontario charges CA$16 per notarized document and CA$32 per Ontario government document, British Columbia CA$20, Alberta CA$25, Saskatchewan CA$50 per certificate, signatory or package, and Quebec CA$66.50 per document.

How long does an apostille take?

It depends on the authority. On October 21, 2026, Global Affairs Canada was processing requests received 25 business days earlier, plus mailing time. Ontario processes mail-in requests within 15 business days.

Can I get an apostille on a photocopy?

Not on a plain photocopy. Global Affairs Canada may authenticate a copy certified by a notary, depending on the document.

Does an apostille work in every country?

Only in countries that belong to the Apostille Convention. Other countries may require legalization at their embassy or consulate after the document is authenticated in Canada.
