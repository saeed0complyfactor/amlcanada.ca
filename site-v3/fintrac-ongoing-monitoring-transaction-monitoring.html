FINTRAC transaction monitoring requirements: ongoing monitoring for MSBs

Guide · Published 19 November 2026 · 7 min read · AML Canada

FINTRAC transaction monitoring requirements for an MSB start with section 123.1 of the Regulations: once you have a business relationship with a client, you must monitor it periodically, at a frequency set by your risk assessment, to detect suspicious transactions and keep client information current. The rules, thresholds and alert reviews that put this into practice come from FINTRAC's guidance and examination manual, and this guide marks which parts are law, guidance or practice.

Key points

  • PCMLTFR s. 123.1 requires periodic, risk-based ongoing monitoring of every business relationship, for four set purposes.
  • High-risk clients need enhanced ongoing monitoring under written procedures (PCMLTFA s. 9.6(3), PCMLTFR s. 157).
  • Monitoring rules, thresholds and alert decisions can be tested in FINTRAC examinations, so tie each one to your risk assessment and write down why.
  • Keep a record of your monitoring measures and what they found for at least five years (PCMLTFR ss. 146(1) and 148(1)(c)).

What are FINTRAC's transaction monitoring requirements?

Two legal duties sit behind transaction monitoring for an MSB. Section 7 of the PCMLTFA requires you to report every completed or attempted transaction where there are reasonable grounds to suspect a link to money laundering, terrorist financing or sanctions evasion. Section 123.1 of the Regulations requires ongoing monitoring of business relationships to detect those transactions.

The Regulations, current to 29 October 2026, don't use the phrase "transaction monitoring" and set no monitoring rules or thresholds. FINTRAC's assessment manual says examiners review your policies and procedures to confirm you have a monitoring process that lets you detect, assess and, when required, report suspicious transactions. Examiners may ask whether that process is automated, manual or both.

The process has to be written down. Section 156(1)(b) of the Regulations requires written compliance policies and procedures that are kept up to date and, for an entity, approved by a senior officer. FINTRAC's STR guidance says those policies must set out how you identify and assess suspicious transactions.

What are the ongoing monitoring requirements for FINTRAC clients?

Under section 123.1 of the Regulations, a business that enters into a business relationship with a client must periodically monitor that relationship, based on its risk assessment. The law gives the monitoring four purposes:

  • detecting transactions you must report under section 7 of the Act;
  • keeping client identification information, beneficial ownership information and the record of the relationship's purpose and intended nature up to date;
  • reassessing the risk of the client's transactions and activities;
  • checking that transactions and activities are consistent with what you know about the client, including your risk assessment of them.

Under section 4.1 of the Regulations, an MSB enters into a business relationship at the earliest of three events: opening an account for the client, the second time it must verify the client's identity, or signing a service agreement with a client that is an entity. For a foreign MSB, the service agreement trigger applies to entities in Canada. FINTRAC's business relationship guidance reads the second-verification trigger as within a 5-year period.

Section 145 then requires a record of the purpose and intended nature of the relationship. As a best practice, FINTRAC says this record should help you anticipate the transactions a client may conduct, so you can spot unusual ones. What goes into the file at onboarding is covered in our MSB onboarding file review checklist.

For entity clients, section 138(2) of the Regulations requires reasonable measures to confirm beneficial ownership information in the course of ongoing monitoring.

How often must an MSB monitor, and what is enhanced ongoing monitoring?

Monitoring frequency follows risk. FINTRAC's ongoing monitoring guidance says low-risk clients may need less frequent monitoring, while high-risk clients require enhanced measures. FINTRAC expects your policies and procedures to state how often you will monitor each client or group of clients, based on your risk assessment.

For high risk, the requirement is law. PCMLTFA s. 9.6(3) requires special measures, and PCMLTFR s. 157(b)(ii) prescribes written policies and procedures for ongoing monitoring at a frequency appropriate to the level of risk. FINTRAC's guidance suggests these methods for enhanced ongoing monitoring:

  • reviewing transactions on an approved schedule that involves management sign-off;
  • reviewing reports of high-risk transactions more often;
  • flagging activity that deviates from your expectations;
  • setting limits or parameters on transactions that trigger early warnings and a mandatory review;
  • reviewing transactions more often against suspicious transaction indicators.

Enhanced monitoring ends when the relationship ends or when your risk assessment no longer rates the client high risk. FINTRAC says ordinary monitoring then continues at the frequency for the client's new rating. How to set those ratings is covered in our FINTRAC risk assessment guide for MSBs.

What are transaction monitoring rules?

Transaction monitoring rules are the automated or manual criteria that flag a transaction or pattern for review, such as an amount, a frequency or a change from a client's expected activity. FINTRAC's assessment manual says examiners confirm that your rules are reasonable, put into practice and monitor transactions in keeping with your risk assessment.

According to the manual, examiners may also:

  • ask how often you review and adjust your rules, what causes a change and how you document it;
  • assess whether you have a reasonable rationale for the thresholds on your rules;
  • assess whether you have enough resources to review transactions for your size and transaction volume;
  • check that you identify and address gaps between a client's actual and expected activity, which the manual calls a common indicator.

A new monitoring system counts as a new technology in FINTRAC's glossary. Section 156(2) of the Regulations requires you to assess and document the risk of a new technology that may affect your clients or services before you introduce it.

In our view, each rule should trace back to a line in your risk assessment and to an indicator in FINTRAC's MSB indicators, with its threshold and rationale written down.

How should alerts be reviewed and documented?

FINTRAC's assessment manual describes alerts as transactions flagged by your internal monitoring because they may relate to money laundering or terrorist financing, and says they should be assessed further under your policies and procedures. Examiners may ask how you prioritize alerts and what process you follow to assess and decide on each one.

Examiners also review the unusual transactions you chose not to report, to confirm those decisions were sound, and check for a sound escalation and decision-making process. FINTRAC's STR guidance says that where an automated system flags transactions, a person may still assess them as a best practice.

The record is a legal duty. PCMLTFR s. 146(1) requires a record of the measures you take in ongoing monitoring and of the information you obtain, and s. 148(1)(c) requires you to keep it for at least five years from the day it was created. FINTRAC says this information is likely to be specific to one relationship, so document it separately from your policies.

In our view, each closed alert should show the rule that fired, who reviewed it, what they checked, the decision and the date.

When does an alert lead to an STR assessment?

An alert becomes an STR question when the review turns up facts, context or indicators that point toward suspicion. The legal test is reasonable grounds to suspect, under section 7 of the Act. FINTRAC's MSB indicators guidance says a single indicator may not appear suspicious on its own, and that observing one can lead you to assess the transaction further.

FINTRAC's STR guidance lists the measures that lead to a decision: screening for suspicious transactions, assessing the facts and context, linking indicators to that assessment, and explaining your grounds in the report. It says the STR is due as soon as practicable after you complete those measures. After a report, FINTRAC says you must keep reporting later transactions while the suspicion remains, and expects you to reassess the client periodically as part of your risk assessment or ongoing monitoring.

Report types, deadlines and the 24-hour rule are covered in our FINTRAC reporting guide. For filing tools, see our FINTRAC reporting software page.

Who does what when the compliance officer is outsourced

When the compliance officer is part-time, monitoring is shared. In our engagements as an outsourced compliance officer, the business's team runs day-to-day monitoring and brings us anything that needs a compliance decision. We aren't watching platforms full time.

  • Your team watches the monitoring platform, turns rules on and off, and releases transactions that don't need our review.
  • Your team escalates transactions and alerts through a shared channel, sends requests for information to customers and handles all customer contact.
  • We help deploy monitoring rules and thresholds, review the transactions and alerts your team escalates, and decide on them.
  • We prepare and file STRs and other FINTRAC reports, and maintain your policies, procedures and risk assessment.
  • Your team or its providers build and integrate the monitoring system. Integration is outside our scope.

Our role is advisory, and following that advice in practice is the business's responsibility. The full escalation workflow is set out in how our outsourced compliance officer service works.

Ongoing and transaction monitoring: common questions

Still not sure? Book a consultation or write to info@amlcanada.ca.

What is the 24-hour rule in FINTRAC?

It is FINTRAC's term for treating multiple transactions made within a 24-hour period as a single transaction for threshold reporting. Our FINTRAC reporting guide explains how it works.

Does FINTRAC require automated transaction monitoring software?

The Regulations don't require it. FINTRAC's assessment manual asks whether your monitoring process is automated, manual or both, and checks that your rules are reasonable, match your risk assessment and are backed by enough resources for your transaction volume.

Do I have to monitor clients who aren't in a business relationship?

The ongoing monitoring duty in PCMLTFR s. 123.1 applies to business relationships. The STR duty in section 7 of the PCMLTFA covers every completed or attempted transaction, so a suspicious one-off transaction still has to be assessed.

How long must ongoing monitoring records be kept?

At least five years from the day each record was created, under PCMLTFR s. 148(1)(c). Section 146(1) requires a record of the monitoring measures you take and the information you obtain.

Can an outsourced compliance officer run our transaction monitoring?

In our model, your team runs day-to-day monitoring and releases transactions that don't need review. We help set the rules and thresholds, decide on the alerts you escalate and file any reports. Building and integrating the system stays with your team or its providers.
