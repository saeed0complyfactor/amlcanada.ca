How to incorporate in Canada as a non-resident

Guide · Published 11 November 2026 · 6 min read · AML Canada

You can incorporate in Canada as a non-resident, and you don't need to live in Canada or hold Canadian citizenship to own the company. The jurisdictions differ on who can sit on the board: a federal corporation needs resident Canadian directors, and Ontario, British Columbia and Alberta have no director residency rule.

Key points

  • A federal (CBCA) corporation needs at least 25% resident Canadian directors, and at least one if it has fewer than four (s. 105(3)).
  • Ontario, British Columbia and Alberta have no resident director rule. Ontario and Alberta repealed theirs; BC's qualifications have none.
  • Every jurisdiction needs a registered office in Canada, and Alberta also needs an agent for service who is a resident Albertan.
  • A company incorporated in Canada after 26 April 1965 is deemed resident in Canada for income tax (ITA s. 250(4)), unless a tax treaty makes it resident elsewhere.

Can a non-resident incorporate a company in Canada?

Yes. As of 11 November 2026, a person with no ties to Canada can incorporate a company federally or in a province. The rule that differs between jurisdictions is director residency. The Canada Business Corporations Act, s. 105(3) requires resident Canadian directors on a federal board. Ontario, British Columbia and Alberta have no such rule.

For a foreign owner, that makes the jurisdiction the first decision. If every director you plan to appoint lives outside Canada, a federal corporation needs at least one more director who is a resident Canadian. A provincial corporation in Ontario, BC or Alberta doesn't.

Corporations Canada says a federal corporation must have at least one director, and that one individual can be the sole shareholder, director and officer. It also says any individual can be an officer.

Director residency rules by jurisdiction

As of 11 November 2026, the federal statute is the only one of these four that requires resident Canadian directors: at least 25% of the board, and at least one director if there are fewer than four. Ontario, British Columbia and Alberta have none. Other provinces aren't shown because we haven't checked their current statute text.

All four statutes allow a private company to have a single director: CBCA s. 102(2), Ontario s. 115(2), BC s. 120 and Alberta s. 101(2). Each sets a higher minimum for public, offering or distributing companies.

Non-resident director rules for a federal corporation

Under CBCA s. 105(3), at least 25% of the directors of a federal corporation must be resident Canadians, and a board of fewer than four needs at least one. Corporations Canada repeats the rule in its guidance on directors. We found no amendment to this subsection in the Act's list of amendments not in force.

Section 2 of the CBCA defines a resident Canadian as an individual who is:

  • a Canadian citizen ordinarily resident in Canada;
  • a Canadian citizen not ordinarily resident in Canada who is a member of a prescribed class of persons; or
  • a permanent resident ordinarily resident in Canada, except one who has been ordinarily resident in Canada for more than one year after first becoming eligible to apply for citizenship.

The rule applies at board meetings too. Under CBCA s. 114(3), directors can't transact business at a meeting unless the required share of resident Canadians is present. Section 114(4) allows an exception when an absent resident Canadian director approves the business in writing or by phone or other communication, and the required number would have been present with that director.

Corporations in sectors with Canadian ownership restrictions need a majority of resident Canadian directors (s. 105(3.1)). Corporations Canada gives airlines, telecommunications, book retailing and film or video distribution as examples.

Ontario, BC and Alberta: no resident director needed

In Ontario, British Columbia and Alberta, every director can live outside Canada. Each province still requires a registered office inside the province.

Ontario

Ontario's Business Corporations Act shows subsection 118(3) as repealed by 2020, c. 34, Sched. 1, s. 5, in force on 5 July 2021. Section 118(1) now disqualifies only people under 18, people found incapable, non-individuals and bankrupts. Section 14(1) requires a registered office in Ontario.

British Columbia

BC's Business Corporations Act, s. 124 lists who can't act as a director: people under 18, people found incapable, undischarged bankrupts and people convicted of certain offences, subject to exceptions. Residency isn't on the list. Section 34(1) requires a registered office and a records office in BC (Part 2).

Alberta

Alberta's Business Corporations Act shows subsection 105(3) as repealed by 2020 c25 s1. Alberta has one extra requirement. Under s. 20.1(1), a corporation must appoint an agent for service who is a resident Albertan, meaning a Canadian citizen or permanent resident ordinarily resident in Alberta (s. 1(cc.1)).

What is a non-resident corporation in Canada?

For income tax, the CRA's page on residency of a corporation says a corporation incorporated in Canada after 26 April 1965 is deemed resident in Canada throughout a tax year, under Income Tax Act s. 250(4). A non-resident corporation is one that isn't resident under those rules, such as a company incorporated abroad whose central management and control is exercised outside Canada.

The CRA says a company is generally resident where its central management and control is exercised, which usually means where the board meets. It also says a corporation can be resident in Canada without being a Canadian corporation. Where a tax treaty makes a corporation resident in another country, s. 250(5) deems it non-resident in Canada. The CRA directs corporations with dual residency questions to the International Tax Section of a tax services office.

If a foreign company doing business in Canada without incorporating here needs a business number or CRA program account, such as GST/HST or payroll, it registers with the CRA as a non-resident. The CRA's non-resident registration page says to use its Non-Resident Business Registration online form if the business is incorporated or located outside Canada, or if you have no SIN or one starting with 0. Tax treatment depends on your facts, so take advice from a tax professional.

Does the Investment Canada Act apply to a new company?

It can. Under the Investment Canada Act, s. 11(a), an investment by a non-Canadian to establish a new Canadian business is subject to notification. Section 12 requires the non-Canadian to notify the Director of Investments before the investment is implemented or within 30 days after.

The Act defines a Canadian business as one carried on in Canada that has a place of business, people employed or self-employed in connection with the business, and assets in Canada used in it. A new Canadian business is one the non-Canadian isn't already carrying on in Canada and that is unrelated to its other Canadian businesses, subject to a cultural-sector exception. Whether your company meets that definition depends on what it will do in Canada.

Registrations and filings after you incorporate

After incorporating, a non-resident owner still has tax, provincial and corporate filings to keep up, and a regulated business needs its own registration.

  • Business number. The CRA says a federal incorporation gives you a business number and a corporation income tax account. Incorporating in Ontario, BC, Alberta and five other listed provinces does too. GST/HST and payroll accounts are registered separately.
  • Provinces where you do business. Corporations Canada says provincial and territorial laws require a federal corporation to register in each province or territory where it conducts business.
  • Annual return and ISC. A federal corporation files an annual return within 60 days of its anniversary date and files information on individuals with significant control at the same time. ISC changes must be filed within 15 days (Corporations Canada).
  • Directors and office. Tell Corporations Canada within 15 days of a change of directors or registered office address. Directors' names and addresses are public.
  • Regulated services. A company that will offer money services must register with FINTRAC before it begins operating. See foreign MSB registration if you'll serve Canada from abroad, or MSB registration for a Canadian company.

Incorporate as a non-resident: common questions

Can a non-resident with no ties to Canada incorporate in Canada?

Yes. Ontario, British Columbia and Alberta have no resident director rule, so a board of non-residents is allowed. The company still needs a registered office in the province, and an Alberta company also needs an agent for service who is a resident Albertan. A federal corporation needs at least one resident Canadian director.

What is a non-resident corporation in Canada?

For income tax, it's a corporation that isn't resident in Canada. The CRA says a corporation incorporated in Canada after 26 April 1965 is deemed resident, and other corporations are generally resident where their central management and control is exercised. A tax treaty can change the result.

Can a non-resident be the only director of a federal corporation?

No. Under CBCA s. 105(3), a federal corporation with fewer than four directors needs at least one resident Canadian director. A sole director must therefore be a resident Canadian.

Can a foreign owner hold all the shares of a Canadian corporation?

The residency rules in the CBCA and the Ontario, BC and Alberta statutes apply to directors, and Corporations Canada says one individual can be the sole shareholder, director and officer. Some sectors restrict Canadian ownership, and a non-Canadian establishing a new Canadian business may have to notify under the Investment Canada Act.
