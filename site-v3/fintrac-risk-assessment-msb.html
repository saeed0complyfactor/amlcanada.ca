FINTRAC risk assessment for MSBs: the rules and a worked example

Guide · Published 11 November 2026 · 7 min read · AML Canada

A FINTRAC risk assessment for MSBs is the written assessment of how your clients, services, delivery channels and locations could expose you to money laundering or terrorist financing. Section 156 of the Regulations lists the factors, and FINTRAC's guidance describes a method. Below, each factor is matched to its legal source and to the evidence an examiner may ask to see.

Key points

  • PCMLTFR s. 156(1)(c) requires every MSB to assess and document its risks across clients, products and channels, geography and other relevant factors.
  • Under s. 156(2), assess a new product, service or technology before you launch it.
  • High risk triggers written enhanced measures under PCMLTFA s. 9.6(3) and PCMLTFR s. 157.
  • FINTRAC also expects you to consider sanctions evasion risk and the latest National Risk Assessment.

What are the requirements for risk assessment under FINTRAC?

Every MSB and foreign MSB must assess and document its money laundering and terrorist financing risks as part of its compliance program. Where it finds the risk high, it must take special measures. The duty comes from section 9.6 of the PCMLTFA and section 156 of the Regulations. FINTRAC's guidance adds detail and doesn't prescribe a method.

As of 11 November 2026, the legal requirements are:

  • PCMLTFA s. 9.6(1.1). Your compliance program must be "reasonably designed, risk-based and effective". S.C. 2026, c. 4, s. 79 added this subsection, in force since 26 March 2026.
  • PCMLTFA s. 9.6(2). The program must include policies and procedures to assess the risk of a money laundering offence or a terrorist activity financing offence.
  • PCMLTFR s. 156(1)(c). You must assess and document that risk, considering your clients and business relationships, your products, services and delivery channels, the geographic location of your activities and any other relevant factor.
  • PCMLTFR s. 156(2). You must assess and document the risk of a new development or new technology before you introduce it.
  • PCMLTFA s. 9.6(3) and PCMLTFR s. 157. Where the risk is high, you need written policies and procedures for enhanced measures to verify identity, keep client information up to date and monitor business relationships, at a frequency that matches the risk.

FINTRAC's compliance program guidance also asks you to assess sanctions evasion risk and to consider your sector's risks in the latest National Risk Assessment. Those two points are FINTRAC expectations. For the other parts of the program, see FINTRAC compliance program requirements.

Which risk factors apply to a money services business?

Five of the six factors in section 156 of the Regulations apply to an MSB: clients and business relationships; products, services and delivery channels; geography; new developments and technologies; and any other relevant factor. The affiliate factor in paragraph 156(1)(c)(iv) covers only entities in paragraphs 5(a) to (g) of the Act, such as banks and securities dealers. MSBs fall under paragraph 5(h), and foreign MSBs under 5(h.1).

FINTRAC says the law doesn't prohibit high-risk activities or business relationships. If you have them, you must document them, put controls in place and apply the prescribed special measures. FINTRAC also says you are accountable for the activities of your agents.

FINTRAC says a table of ratings like this is not in itself a risk assessment. If you use a checklist, you must be able to provide a documented analysis that draws conclusions on your vulnerabilities and the threats you face.

How to do an AML risk assessment

FINTRAC suggests a six-step cycle that you can adapt to your business: identify inherent risks, set your risk tolerance, create controls, evaluate the risk that remains, implement the approach and review it. The steps are FINTRAC guidance, while the documentation and special measures inside them are required by law.

  1. Identify inherent risks. Inherent risk is the risk before controls. Rate each factor and write down why. FINTRAC says the law doesn't require a low, medium and high scale, but you must set a scale that suits your size and type. A very small business may only need low and high.
  2. Set your risk tolerance. Decide which regulatory, reputational, legal or financial risks you will accept. FINTRAC expects senior management to approve it, where you have senior management. A high tolerance doesn't remove your obligations.
  3. Create controls. Mitigate every high-risk element and put the written mitigation strategies in your policies and procedures.
  4. Evaluate residual risk. If the risk left after controls exceeds your tolerance, return to step 3.
  5. Implement. Apply the approach as documented, including monitoring frequencies and special measures.
  6. Review. Test it in your two-year effectiveness review, and update it when your business model changes.

Business-wide and client risk assessments

FINTRAC's model splits the work into a business-based assessment of the whole business and a relationship-based assessment of your clients, and you can do both at once. The business-wide risk assessment covers every factor except clients and relationships. The client assessment looks at the services each client uses, their geography and their patterns of activity.

You can rate clients individually or in logical groups. FINTRAC says one high-risk indicator doesn't necessarily make a client high risk, apart from three indicators it names. If you have no business relationships, you don't need a client worksheet for low and medium risk clients, but FINTRAC says to include high-risk ones, such as clients named in a suspicious transaction report you sent.

What the 2025 National Risk Assessment says about MSBs

The Department of Finance published the 2025 Assessment of Money Laundering and Terrorist Financing Risks in Canada, also called the National Risk Assessment, in August 2025. It rates the inherent vulnerability of the money services business sector as medium to very high, alongside crypto assets, corporations, express trusts and domestic systemically important banks in its very high group.

  • The report names retail multi-service MSBs and MSBs using informal value transfer systems, such as hawalas, as the most vulnerable types.
  • It rates crypto assets as very high, and payment service providers and crowdfunding platforms as high.
  • It says newer MSB sub-sectors, including virtual currency dealers, may not yet have the strong understanding of obligations expected of core MSB services regulated since 2008.

The report doesn't assess risks specific to individual organizations or regions. Use it to check that your assessment covers the risks it names for your sub-sector.

What FINTRAC examiners look for

FINTRAC's assessment manual says examiners check that you have a documented risk assessment covering each applicable element and reflecting the type, nature, size and complexity of your business. They focus on whether you rated every aspect of your business, gave reasons and applied special measures to high-risk areas. They may:

  • test a sample of client and transaction records against your ratings and your policies and procedures;
  • check that your controls, policies, training and two-year review address your higher-risk areas;
  • check that you assessed new developments and technologies before introducing them;
  • interview the people responsible for your risk assessment;
  • verify that your assessment considers any ministerial directive covering a jurisdiction or entity you deal with.

If you rate something lower than FINTRAC would, its guidance says you must be able to give a reasonable rationale. Our examination preparation guide covers the wider process.

When should an MSB update its risk assessment?

The law requires you to assess and document the risk before you launch a new development or technology, under section 156(2) of the Regulations. FINTRAC's guidance also says to reassess regularly and to document the risks of each change, such as a new product, service, technology or location.

FINTRAC counts systems used in your own AML controls as new technologies, such as a transaction monitoring system or a client onboarding or identification tool. It lists acquisitions, business model changes and restructuring as new developments.

Your two-year effectiveness review must test the risk assessment along with your policies and training. Our effectiveness review checklist lists the questions to ask.

Risk assessment for MSBs: common questions

Does FINTRAC require a specific risk assessment template?

No. FINTRAC says it doesn't prescribe how a risk assessment should be conducted, and its example worksheets are optional. The assessment must be documented, and FINTRAC expects it to reflect your business and include all the prescribed elements.

Is a business-wide risk assessment a legal requirement?

Yes. PCMLTFR s. 156(1)(c) requires you to assess and document risk across your clients, products, services, delivery channels, geography and other relevant factors. The terms business-based and relationship-based come from FINTRAC's guidance.

Do MSBs have to assess sanctions evasion risk?

FINTRAC's compliance program guidance asks you to assess sanctions evasion risk alongside money laundering and terrorist financing. As of 11 November 2026, PCMLTFA s. 9.6(2) refers only to money laundering and terrorist activity financing offences.

How often should an MSB update its risk assessment?

Before any new development or technology (a legal requirement) and whenever your risks change (a FINTRAC expectation). Your effectiveness review must also test it at least every two years.

Can a consultant prepare our risk assessment?

Yes. FINTRAC says that if a service provider performs your risk assessment, you remain ultimately responsible for meeting the obligation.
