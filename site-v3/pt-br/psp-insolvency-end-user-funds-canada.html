PSP insolvency: what happens to end-user funds in Canada

Guide · Published 3 November 2026 · 8 min read · AML Canada

In a PSP insolvency in Canada, the end-user funds a payment service provider holds are meant to be paid to end users as soon as feasible, because section 20 of the RPAA requires those funds to be held in trust or covered by insurance or a guarantee. Whether that works depends on the provider's ledger, its shortfall checks and its written procedures for returning funds.

Pontos principais

  • RPAA s. 20 and RPAR s. 15 aim to pay end-user funds back as soon as feasible after an RPAR s. 14(3) insolvency event.
  • Deposit insurance at the PSP's bank does not pay out when the PSP itself becomes insolvent.
  • The ledger and a final reconciliation show who is owed what, and every shortfall must be found, investigated and fixed.
  • End users can check the Bank's registry, the provider's safeguarding terms and their own contact details.

How does RPAA safeguarding protect funds in a PSP insolvency?

RPAA safeguarding is meant to keep end-user funds protected and payable to end users if their payment service provider (PSP) becomes insolvent. The means come from section 20(1) of the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA). The insolvency rules sit in sections 13 to 17 of the Retail Payment Activities Regulations (RPAR).

As of 3 November 2026, a PSP holds the funds in trust in a trust account (s. 20(1)(a)), or in an account covered by insurance or a guarantee worth at least the balance (s. 20(1)(c)). Either account must not be used for any other purpose. The Bank of Canada's safeguarding guideline (December 2024) says no other method is prescribed under s. 20(1)(b). Section 20(3) stops the account provider from asserting set-off or compensation against the funds.

RPAR s. 15(1) gives the safeguarding framework two purposes: reliable access without delay, and payment to end users as soon as feasible after an insolvency event. Both objectives came up again when Bank staff spoke at the Bank's supervision update on 2 November 2026. Our RPAA safeguarding guide covers the day-to-day rules.

What counts as an insolvency event under RPAR s. 14(3)?

Under RPAR s. 14(3), an insolvency event occurs when a PSP brings an insolvency proceeding against itself, consents to one, or reaches 30 days after another person brings one, unless that proceeding is discontinued or dismissed within those 30 days. RPAR s. 14(4) covers any bankruptcy, insolvency, liquidation, dissolution or winding-up process, under the law of any jurisdiction.

The same event is used in four places:

  • insurance or guarantee proceeds become payable for end users as soon as feasible (RPAR s. 14(2)(b));
  • the framework must aim to pay end users as soon as feasible after it (RPAR s. 15(1)(b));
  • the PSP must find any instance where funds would not have been payable had it occurred (RPAR s. 16(1));
  • the Bank's incident notification guideline (paragraph 3.1.3) lists it as an example of a material incident.

Guideline paragraph 3.26 says a PSP subject to such an event must notify the Bank under RPAA s. 18. Under the Bank's incident guideline, the PSP has 48 hours at most from its materiality decision to file. See our guide to RPAA incident reporting.

Trust account or insured account: what differs in an insolvency?

A trust account and an insured or guaranteed account are both accepted under RPAA s. 20(1), and both need a segregated safeguarding account. They protect end users through different legal routes. Deposit insurance alone does not meet the Act.

The Bank lets a PSP combine the two accepted means (guideline 2.2). Under RPAA s. 20(2), a PSP that itself takes provincially insured deposits does not safeguard funds held as those deposits. That exception does not cover a PSP that only banks at a member institution (guideline 7.5).

Neither the Act nor the guideline says how a court or trustee would treat a particular arrangement in a bankruptcy. The Bank expects PSPs to get legal advice on trust validity and on complex cross-border cases (guideline 2.21 to 2.25). Ask a Canadian insolvency lawyer about your own structure.

Why is the ledger the record of who is owed what?

The ledger shows how much a PSP owes each end user. The Bank expects an insolvency trustee to be able to use it to work out what to return to each person (guideline 3.28.1). Under RPAR s. 15(2)(b), the ledger records every end user whose funds are held, how to contact them, and what the PSP holds for them at the close of each day.

The guideline adds that the ledger should:

  • separate funds in the safeguarding account from funds not yet placed there, and show when funds are in trust (paragraph 3.7);
  • be updated every day, with real-time updates as best practice (paragraph 3.9);
  • hold current contact details so funds can be returned accurately (paragraphs 3.11 and 3.12);
  • record funds in transit, which the PSP has an instruction to send but has not sent (paragraph 3.28.1).

At the 2 November 2026 session, Bank staff described the ledger as "more than just a record keeping tool." They said the Bank expects PSPs to trace funds to the specific safeguarding account that holds them.

What happens to a shortfall if the PSP fails?

A shortfall is money that should be safeguarded but is not. RPAR s. 16(1), headed "Evaluation of insolvency protection", requires a PSP to find, as soon as feasible, any instance where end-user funds would not have been payable to end users had an insolvency event occurred.

Guideline paragraphs 4.2 to 4.8 make this a quantitative test. Compare funds safeguarded, or insurance cover, with funds held. Check daily as best practice and fix gaps in a timely manner. Investigate the root cause immediately (RPAR s. 16(2)) and report each shortfall in the annual report (RPAR s. 19(3)(c)). Funds not placed on receipt because of processing constraints count as shortfalls.

Bank staff said at the session that shortfalls can start in settlement and processing, in reconciliation or ledger errors, in failed systems or third parties, and in unauthorized transactions or returned payments.

On insolvency, the procedures should give a final reconciliation of funds held, funds safeguarded and any shortfall, with the trustee's options to address it (paragraph 3.30). The procedures should also tell end users whose funds were not safeguarded how they can advance claims (paragraph 3.29). The guideline does not say how those claims rank against other creditors, so get legal advice.

What must procedures to return funds contain?

Under RPAR s. 15(2)(c), the framework must explain how a trustee or insurer can reach all relevant records, contact end users as soon as feasible, and fix ledger errors and shortfalls. It must also set out the steps to return funds and the role of agents and third-party service providers.

Guideline paragraph 3.29 expects the procedures to say how the insurer will learn of the event and who contacts end users. They should also set out the instructions end users get, the payout methods they can choose, and how a trustee can reach funds at a financial institution. Paragraph 3.31 adds how agents are told to stop taking funds.

The Bank's FAQ says its role does not include administering claims or notifying insurers after an insolvency event. Bank staff said at the 2 November 2026 update that procedures should set out concrete steps and be "clear, comprehensive, and executable." Our guide to RPAA compliance frameworks shows where they sit.

We also suggest a desk walk-through: ask someone outside the safeguarding team to return funds on paper using only your records. The Bank has not prescribed a test format. Guideline 3.3 says the framework should suit the people who will use it.

What can end users check about a provider holding their money?

If you keep a balance with a payment app or other PSP, check that it is registered with the Bank of Canada, that its terms say how your money is safeguarded, and that it has your current contact details.

  • Search the Bank's registry of payment service providers. As of 3 November 2026, it shows each PSP's payment functions, including whether it holds funds. The Bank also lists refused or revoked registrations and notes published violations on registry entries.
  • Read registration for what it is. The Bank's FAQ says it does not give PSPs a licence. Bank staff also said at the 2 November 2026 session that a registered PSP did not have to demonstrate compliance before it was registered.
  • Read the terms. A PSP should tell you which of your funds are safeguarded (guideline 1.4.1) and when funds may not be protected straight away on receipt (paragraph 2.7.3).
  • Don't rely on deposit insurance. CDIC or provincial cover at the PSP's bank does not pay out when the PSP itself fails.
  • Keep your email and phone number current, since that is how a trustee would reach you. Save statements showing your balance.

If a provider fails, read its notices and follow the trustee's instructions. The Bank does not administer claims, and a lawyer can advise on your position. PSPs can have these procedures built into their safeguarding framework through our RPAA compliance program.

PSP insolvency and end-user funds: common questions

What happens to my money if a payment app goes bankrupt in Canada?

If the app is a PSP holding your funds, RPAA s. 20 requires them to be held in trust or covered by insurance or a guarantee, and its procedures must aim to return them as soon as feasible. For any amount that was not safeguarded, the procedures should tell you how to make a claim.

Is money in a payment app covered by CDIC?

Not against the app's own failure. The Bank of Canada's FAQ says deposit insurance protects the PSP as accountholder if its bank fails, and is not paid out when the PSP itself becomes insolvent.

Does the Bank of Canada pay customers back if a PSP becomes insolvent?

No. The Bank's FAQ says its role does not include administering claims or notifying insurers. The PSP's safeguarding framework must explain how a trustee or insurer will return funds.

What is an insolvency event under the RPAR?

Under RPAR s. 14(3), it is a PSP bringing an insolvency proceeding against itself, consenting to one, or 30 days passing after someone else brings one that is not discontinued or dismissed.

How often must a PSP review its insolvency procedures?

At least once a year as part of the safeguarding framework review under RPAR s. 15(6), and after changes to its safeguarding means, accounts or insurance. An independent review is also needed at least every three years under s. 17.

RPAA

Salvaguarda dos fundos dos usuários finais na RPAA

Desde 8 de setembro de 2025, um prestador de serviços de pagamento que mantém fundos de seus usuários deve salvaguardá-los, seja em fideicomisso (trust) em uma conta fiduciária, seja em uma conta segregada respaldada por seguro ou garantia. Ele também precisa de uma estrutura de salvaguarda por escrito e de uma revisão independente pelo menos a cada três anos.

Comunicação de incidentes na RPAA e a regra das 48 horas

Quando um prestador de serviços de pagamento toma conhecimento de um incidente com impacto relevante, deve notificar sem demora o Banco do Canadá e os usuários finais, prestadores de serviços de pagamento e câmaras de compensação afetados. O Banco espera a primeira notificação em no máximo 48 horas após o prestador concluir que o incidente é relevante.

As estruturas de compliance da RPAA de que um PSP precisa

Todo prestador de serviços de pagamento precisa de uma estrutura escrita de gestão de riscos e de resposta a incidentes, e o que mantém fundos de usuários finais também precisa de uma estrutura de proteção de fundos. Terceiros, agentes e o papel do executivo sênior estão incorporados a esses dois documentos. As regras de manutenção de registros abrangem ambos, e o relatório anual, as notificações de alterações e as notificações de incidentes se baseiam neles.
