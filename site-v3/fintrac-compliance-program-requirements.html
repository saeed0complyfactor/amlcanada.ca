FINTRAC compliance program requirements

Guide · Published 16 October 2026 · 6 min read · AML Canada

Every business FINTRAC regulates must establish and implement a compliance program. It has five parts: a compliance officer, written policies and procedures, a risk assessment, an ongoing training program and plan, and a review of the program at least every two years.

Key points

  • All reporting entities need a compliance program, including MSBs and virtual currency dealers.
  • Policies and procedures must be written, kept up to date and, for an entity, approved by a senior officer.
  • Assess the risks of a new development or new technology before you introduce it.
  • Test the program at least every two years. If your business is an entity, report the results in writing to a senior officer within 30 days.

What a FINTRAC compliance program is

A compliance program, sometimes called an AML/ATF program, is the set of people, documents and processes a reporting entity uses to meet its obligations under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA). FINTRAC's compliance program guidance says it forms the basis for meeting all of your reporting, record keeping, client identification and other know-your-client requirements. Every reporting entity must have one, whatever its size.

FINTRAC's guidance cites subsection 9.6(1) of the PCMLTFA and section 156 of the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Regulations as the legal basis.

1. Appoint a compliance officer

In a small business, the compliance officer can be the owner, the operator or a senior manager. For a larger business, FINTRAC's example is someone at a senior level with direct access to senior management and the board of directors. As a best practice, the compliance officer of a larger business shouldn't be directly involved in receiving, transferring or paying out funds.

FINTRAC says the officer needs the authority and resources to implement the program, knowledge of how your business works, knowledge of the money laundering, terrorist financing and sanctions evasion risks in your sector, and an understanding of your sector's requirements under the PCMLTFA. They can delegate tasks, but they stay responsible for implementing the program, and your business stays responsible for meeting the requirements.

FINTRAC's guidance says the compliance officer can be you or another individual. If you're weighing an outsourced compliance officer against an internal appointment, our guide to outsourced vs in-house compliance officers compares the two options.

2. Write policies and procedures

Your policies and procedures must be written, kept up to date when the law or your own processes change, and approved by a senior officer if your business is an entity such as a corporation. Make them available to everyone authorized to act for you, including employees, agents and anyone else who deals with clients or transactions.

FINTRAC expects them to cover, as they apply to you:

  • the compliance program itself;
  • know your client, including identity verification, politically exposed persons, heads of international organizations and their family members and close associates, beneficial ownership and third party determination;
  • business relationships and ongoing monitoring;
  • record keeping;
  • transaction reporting;
  • the travel rule for electronic funds and virtual currency transfers;
  • ministerial directives.

For each obligation, describe when it's triggered, what must be reported or recorded, the steps you take and the deadlines. Where the law asks for "reasonable measures", write down the steps you'll take. If an industry association gave you a generic set of policies, FINTRAC says you must tailor them to your business.

3. Assess your risks

You must assess and document your money laundering, terrorist financing and sanctions evasion risks in the course of your activities. FINTRAC lists the factors to consider:

  • your clients, business relationships and any correspondent banking relationships, including their activity patterns and locations;
  • your products, services and delivery channels;
  • the places where you carry out your activities;
  • new developments or technologies, assessed before you introduce them;
  • if you are a financial entity, life insurance company or securities dealer, the risks from the activities of your affiliates;
  • the risks for your sector in the latest National Risk Assessment of Money Laundering and Terrorist Financing in Canada;
  • any other relevant factor, such as employee turnover.

An MSB that plans to add virtual currency services, for example, must assess the risks of that new service before it launches.

Enhanced measures for high risk

Where you consider a risk high, you must take enhanced measures and set them out in written policies and procedures. They include extra steps to verify identity, updating client information and monitoring business relationships at a frequency that matches the risk. FINTRAC's examples include getting more information on the client, on their source of funds or source of wealth, or on the reasons for a transaction. FINTRAC's risk assessment guidance explains how to build the assessment.

4. Train the people who act for you

If you have employees, agents, mandataries or others authorized to act for you, you need a written, ongoing compliance training program and a documented plan for delivering it. The training should cover your obligations under the PCMLTFA, background on money laundering and terrorist financing, how your business could be used for them, your own policies and procedures, and each person's role when something looks suspicious.

The plan should say who gets trained, which topics and materials you use, how you deliver the training and how often. FINTRAC says recipients should include anyone who deals with clients, is involved in client transactions or handles cash, funds or virtual currency for you, and those who implement or oversee the program, such as the compliance officer, senior management, IT staff and internal auditors. Keep a record of each session, with the date, the attendees and the topics. A sole proprietor with no employees, agents or other authorized persons doesn't need a training program or plan. Our AML training is built around these requirements.

5. Review the program every two years

An effectiveness review tests whether your policies and procedures, risk assessment, and training program and plan work in practice. You must start each review no later than 24 months from the start of the previous one, and finish the previous review before you start the next. The review must be carried out, and its results documented, by an internal or external auditor, or by you if you have no auditor. Whoever does it should know your requirements under the PCMLTFA. As a best practice, the reviewer shouldn't be directly involved in your compliance program activities.

You also need a documented plan for the review that covers every part of the program. It should set out the scope, the reasons for the areas you focus on, the period reviewed, your evaluation methods and sample sizes. The review itself should record when it was done, the period covered, who did it, the test results and the conclusions, including any deficiencies, recommendations and action plans. If your business is an entity, you must report the findings, any other updates to your policies and procedures and the status of those updates, in writing to a senior officer within 30 days of finishing the review. Our effectiveness review checklist sets out what to test, and an external effectiveness review gives you an independent view.

A quick self-check

These questions follow FINTRAC's guidance. If you answer no to any of them, that part of your program needs work.

  • Has your compliance officer been appointed, and do they have the authority and resources to do the job?
  • Are your policies and procedures written, kept up to date, approved by a senior officer if your business is an entity, and tailored to your business?
  • Do they cover the travel rule and the ministerial directives in force?
  • Does your risk assessment describe the products, channels and countries you deal with today?
  • Can you show training records for everyone FINTRAC says should be trained, including senior management and the compliance officer?
  • Do you know when your next effectiveness review must start?

If you're about to register, our compliance program documents are written to be in place before you start operating. If you've just registered, see what to do in your first 90 days.

Compliance program requirements: common questions

Still not sure? Book a consultation or write to info@amlcanada.ca.

Who needs a FINTRAC compliance program?

Every reporting entity under the PCMLTFA must establish and implement one, whatever its size. That includes money services businesses, foreign MSBs and virtual currency dealers.

Can I use a compliance program template?

You can start from one, but FINTRAC says generic policies and procedures must be tailored to your business. The level of detail depends on your size, structure, complexity and exposure to money laundering, terrorist financing and sanctions evasion risks.

Who approves the compliance policies and procedures?

If your business is an entity, such as a corporation, a senior officer must approve them. They must also be kept up to date when the law or your processes change.

Does a sole proprietor need a training program?

No, if you have no employees, agents, mandataries or other people authorized to act for you. You still need the other parts of the program.

How often must the compliance program be reviewed?

At least every two years. Each review must start no later than 24 months from the start of the previous one, and an entity must report the results in writing to a senior officer within 30 days of finishing.
