The travel rule and ministerial directives: what Canadian MSBs must do

Guide · Published 21 October 2026 · 5 min read · AML Canada

Canada's travel rule requires money services businesses to send originator and beneficiary information with funds and virtual currency transfers, and to check it arrives with transfers they receive. Ministerial directives add stricter measures for transactions connected to Russia, Iran and North Korea.

Key points

  • Include names, addresses and account or reference numbers with transfers you send.
  • Take reasonable measures to get missing information, then follow written risk-based procedures.
  • Three ministerial directives are in force: Russia, Iran and North Korea (DPRK).
  • Directive transactions are high risk at any amount, and Iran-connected transactions must all be reported.

What the travel rule is

The travel rule is the requirement to make sure specific information goes with an electronic funds transfer (EFT) or a virtual currency transfer. It comes from section 9.5 of the Act and sections 124 and 124.1 of the Regulations. FINTRAC's guidance applies it to financial entities, money services businesses (MSBs), foreign MSBs and casinos.

Information you receive under the travel rule can't be removed from a transfer. The travel rule isn't a separate record keeping requirement, though following it helps you meet your record keeping and reporting obligations. The EFTs covered are international EFTs, and EFTs within Canada sent as SWIFT MT-103 messages or their equivalent.

Electronic funds transfers

The rule applies to the EFTs you must keep a record of. For an MSB, section 36 of the Regulations requires records when it initiates an EFT of $1,000 or more, sends an international EFT of $1,000 or more initiated by someone else, or is the final recipient of an international EFT of $1,000 or more. FINTRAC's record keeping guidance has the detail.

When you initiate an EFT, include:

  • the name, address and account number or other reference number, if any, of the person or entity who requested the transfer;
  • the beneficiary's name and address; and
  • if applicable, the beneficiary's account number or other reference number.

When you receive an EFT, as an intermediary or as the final recipient, take reasonable measures to make sure that information is included. If you pass an EFT on, include the information you received or obtained.

Virtual currency transfers

MSBs keep records when they transfer $1,000 or more in virtual currency at a client's request, or receive $1,000 or more in virtual currency for remittance to a beneficiary. For those transfers, you must send the name, address and account or other reference number, if any, of both the person who requested the transfer and the beneficiary. For transfers you receive, take reasonable measures to make sure the same information is included. Crypto businesses can read this with our guide to crypto registration with FINTRAC.

When the information is missing

If a transfer arrives without the required information, you must take reasonable measures to obtain it. FINTRAC describes reasonable measures as steps taken to achieve an outcome even if they don't achieve it, such as asking the client, searching open sources, retrieving information you already hold, or consulting commercially available information.

You also need written, risk-based policies and procedures for what happens when those measures fail. FINTRAC says they must cover when you allow, suspend or reject the transfer, and what follow-up measures you take. Write the steps down in your procedures and keep a record of what you did for each case. If the circumstances give you reasonable grounds to suspect money laundering, terrorist financing or sanctions evasion, the usual suspicious transaction reporting duty applies as well.

Under the penalty regulations, the travel rule obligations are minor violations. For violations on or after March 26, 2026, a minor violation can carry up to $40,000 each. Our guide to FINTRAC penalties explains the classes.

What a ministerial directive is

Part 1.1 of the Act, in force since June 19, 2014, lets the Minister of Finance issue directives requiring reporting entities to apply countermeasures to transactions coming from or going to designated foreign jurisdictions or entities. FINTRAC's directives page says FINTRAC informs reporting entities when one is issued, that each directive stays in force until revoked, suspended or amended, and that directives are reviewed at least every three years. Every MSB and foreign MSB must comply (section 11.43).

The three directives in force

DirectiveIn force sinceLatest change
RussiaFebruary 24, 2024FINTRAC guidance updated March 22, 2025
IranJuly 25, 2020Directive amended February 15, 2024 and November 15, 2025; guidance updated June 23, 2026
North Korea (DPRK)December 9, 2017FINTRAC guidance updated March 22, 2025

For any transaction originating from or bound for one of these countries, whatever the amount, each directive requires you to:

  • treat it as high risk;
  • verify the identity of any person or entity requesting or benefiting from it;
  • apply customer due diligence with particular attention to sanctions evasion risk, including the source of funds or virtual currency, the purpose of the transaction, and the beneficial ownership or control of any entity involved; and
  • keep a record of it, even below the usual record keeping thresholds.

The Iran and North Korea directives also require policies and procedures to assess the sanctions evasion risk associated with that country. The Iran directive goes further: every Iran-connected transaction must be reported to FINTRAC. Below-threshold EFTs and virtual currency receipts go on the usual EFT or large virtual currency transaction report, marked "IR2020" in the ministerial directive field. Transfers within Canada go on a suspicious transaction report, also marked "IR2020", with the suspicion and action taken sections left blank.

Deciding whether a transaction is connected

FINTRAC's guidance gives examples of a connection: an origin or destination address in the country, transactions involving the country's government representatives, and use of the Russian ruble or Iranian rial. For Iran, it also lists funds from the sale of an asset in Iran and online transactions traced to an IP address geolocated in Iran. A transfer routed through a third country is caught when details such as the account or third-party information point back to the country.

A client's passport or nationality alone isn't a connection, and neither is a past history of sending funds there, if nothing else about the transaction points to the country.

Building it into your program

Your policies and procedures should cover the travel rule and the ministerial directives, as FINTRAC's compliance program guidance expects. They should also say how you learn of new directives and how you decide a transaction is connected. Your risk assessment should record your exposure to these countries. Failing to comply with a directive is a very serious violation, and knowingly contravening one can also be prosecuted under section 74 of the Act. FINTRAC also publishes advisories on countries identified by the Financial Action Task Force. The most recent on its news page is dated July 15, 2026.

Our 14-document compliance program includes a Travel Rule Policy and Procedure, and our guide to compliance program requirements covers the rest. An outsourced compliance officer can handle directive reviews and reporting. Travel rule and directives: common questions

Still not sure? Book a consultation or write to info@amlcanada.ca.

What is the travel rule in Canada?

It requires financial entities, MSBs, foreign MSBs and casinos to include the requester's and beneficiary's names, addresses and account or reference numbers with electronic funds and virtual currency transfers, and to take reasonable measures to check that information arrives with transfers they receive.

Does the travel rule apply to crypto in Canada?

Yes. MSBs and foreign MSBs must include travel rule information with virtual currency transfers they're required to keep records of, generally $1,000 or more, and take reasonable measures for transfers they receive.

What should I do if a transfer arrives without originator information?

Take reasonable measures to obtain it, such as asking the client. If that fails, follow your written risk-based procedures on whether to allow, suspend or reject the transfer, and record your follow-up.

Which countries do FINTRAC ministerial directives cover?

Three directives are in force: Russia (since February 24, 2024), Iran (since July 25, 2020, amended in 2024 and 2025) and North Korea (since December 9, 2017).

Do MSBs have to report every transaction connected to Iran?

Yes. Under the Iran directive, every transaction originating from or bound for Iran must be reported to FINTRAC, whatever the amount, using the report types FINTRAC's guidance sets out.
