FINTRAC administrative monetary penalties (AMPs) explained

Guide · Published 21 October 2026 · 5 min read · AML Canada

FINTRAC can impose administrative monetary penalties for breaches of the anti-money laundering law. Amendments in force since March 26, 2026 raised the maximum amounts up to 40 times and added compliance agreements and compliance orders.

Key points

  • For violations on or after March 26, 2026, the cap is $4 million per violation for a person and $20 million for an entity.
  • Each violation is classed as minor, serious or very serious, and the class sets the range.
  • You have 30 days to pay or make representations, and paying means accepting the violation.
  • Imposed penalties are made public, and a compliance agreement now follows every prescribed violation.

What an AMP is

An administrative monetary penalty (AMP) is a civil penalty for not complying with the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act and its regulations. FINTRAC has been able to impose them since December 30, 2008. Under the Act, penalties are meant to encourage compliance rather than to punish (section 73.11). A violation isn't an offence (section 73.23), and due diligence is a defence (section 73.24).

FINTRAC can also refer non-compliance to law enforcement as a criminal offence. It can't do both for the same act or omission (section 73.12). FINTRAC's policy says that after an assessment it may take no further action, follow up, issue a penalty, or disclose information to law enforcement.

Penalty amounts in force today

The Strengthening Canada's Immigration System and Borders Act (Bill C-12) received Royal Assent on March 26, 2026. It amended the Act and the Administrative Monetary Penalties Regulations the same day. The new framework applies to violations committed on or after that day. Violations committed before it stay under the former rules, according to the Act's transitional provisions and FINTRAC's notice on the changes.

ClassRange per violation before March 26, 2026Range per violation from March 26, 2026
Minor$1 to $1,000$1 to $40,000
Serious$1 to $100,000$1 to $4,000,000
Very serious$1 to $100,000 (person); $1 to $500,000 (entity)$1 to $20,000,000, with a $4,000,000 cap for a person

Section 73.1 of the Act now caps each prescribed violation at $4,000,000 for a person and $20,000,000 for an entity. The total for all violations on one notice is capped at the greater of $4,000,000 or 3% of a person's gross global income, or the greater of $20,000,000 or 3% of an entity's gross global revenue. For an entity in an affiliated group, the group's revenue is used.

FINTRAC says it scopes each examination so the review period falls entirely under one framework.

Minor, serious and very serious

The schedule to the penalty regulations classifies each violation. Some examples:

  • Very serious: failing to report a suspicious transaction; failing to comply with a ministerial directive; failing to keep a compliance program that is reasonably designed, risk-based and effective; and the compliance program elements in section 156 of the Regulations, such as the compliance officer, policies, risk assessment, training and the two-year review.
  • Serious: MSB registration failures, including not registering, not reporting changes, not answering clarification requests, not renewing and not notifying cessation.
  • Minor: most record keeping, client identification and threshold reporting items, and the travel rule obligations.

Bill C-12 moved certain compliance program violations from serious to very serious, as FINTRAC's changes page notes. FINTRAC publishes harm done guides for seven groups of violations: compliance program, threshold reports (large cash, electronic funds transfer and casino disbursement), suspicious transaction reports, know your client, record keeping, MSB registration, and other compliance measures.

How the amount is set

Section 73.11 of the Act lists the criteria: the non-punitive purpose, the harm done by the violation, and, new since March 26, 2026, the ability of the person or entity to pay. The regulations add the history of compliance. If FINTRAC asks for information on your ability to pay, your later representations can generally rely only on what you gave before its deadline (section 73.111).

FINTRAC's published policy works in two steps. First, it assesses harm: how far the violation interferes with the Act's objectives or FINTRAC's mandate, including resulting harm such as unreported transactions caused by a gap in procedures. For a complete failure to meet a requirement, the base penalty is typically the maximum for that violation. Second, it adjusts for compliance history and the non-punitive purpose. FINTRAC says it's updating this policy, with an updated approach to calculating penalties under the new framework.

From notice of violation to final decision

  1. Notice of violation. It names the violations, the proposed penalty and how it was calculated. FINTRAC must start proceedings within two years after it becomes aware of the subject matter.
  2. Pay, or make representations. You have 30 days after service, or a longer period FINTRAC specifies. Paying means you're deemed to have committed the violation, and proceedings end.
  3. No response. You're deemed to have committed the violation, and FINTRAC imposes the proposed penalty.
  4. Director's decision. If you make written representations, the Director decides on a balance of probabilities and may impose the proposed penalty, a lesser one or none.
  5. Federal Court. For a serious or very serious violation, or a compliance order violation, you can appeal within 30 days after the decision is served. If no decision comes within 90 days after your representations are complete, you can appeal within the following 30 days.

Decisions on minor violations can't be appealed, unless a series of minor violations on one notice counts as serious. The current regulations set that threshold at $400,000 in total. Penalties not paid on time accrue interest and are a debt to the Crown.

Compliance agreements and compliance orders

After proceedings on a prescribed violation end, FINTRAC must require a compliance agreement (section 73.16). It sets out the measures you'll take and a deadline. FINTRAC can extend the deadline by up to one year if you're making substantial progress. If you don't sign within six months, you're deemed to have refused.

If you refuse, or miss the deadline, the Director must make a compliance order and make it public (section 73.17). The order requires you to publish the measures you're taking. Breaching it is a compliance order violation, capped at the greater of $5,000,000 or 3% of gross global income for a person, and the greater of $30,000,000 or 3% of gross global revenue for an entity.

Public naming

When a violation is deemed committed, or the Director decides one was committed, FINTRAC must publish the name of the person or entity, the nature of the violation and the penalty amount, and it may include its reasons (section 73.22). Notices stay on FINTRAC's public notice page for five years. For an MSB, an unpaid penalty also has a registration effect: 30 days after proceedings end, it makes the business ineligible to register, as our guide to revoked registrations explains.

Lowering your risk

Most violations trace back to gaps an internal check would find. Test your program with a regular effectiveness review, fix late or missing reports before FINTRAC calls, and prepare as our examination guide describes. An outsourced compliance officer can keep reporting and records current, and an external effectiveness review gives an independent view. Payment businesses should also check their travel rule and directive procedures, since breaching a directive is very serious.

FINTRAC penalties (AMPs): common questions

Still not sure? Book a consultation or write to info@amlcanada.ca.

What is the maximum FINTRAC penalty now?

For violations on or after March 26, 2026, up to $4 million per violation for a person and $20 million for an entity. The total on one notice can reach the greater of those amounts or 3% of gross global income or revenue.

Does paying a FINTRAC penalty mean admitting the violation?

Yes. Under the Act, paying the penalty in a notice of violation means you're deemed to have committed the violation, and the proceedings end.

Can I appeal a FINTRAC penalty?

First make written representations to FINTRAC's Director within 30 days of the notice. You can then appeal the Director's decision to the Federal Court within 30 days for serious, very serious and compliance order violations.

Will FINTRAC publish my business's name?

Yes, when a violation is deemed committed or the Director decides one was committed. FINTRAC publishes the name, the nature of the violation and the penalty, and notices stay online for five years.

Does FINTRAC consider whether a business can afford the penalty?

Since March 26, 2026, the Act lists ability to pay as one of the criteria for setting the amount, along with the harm done and the purpose of encouraging compliance.
