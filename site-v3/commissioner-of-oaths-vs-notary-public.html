Commissioner of oaths vs notary public: what each can do in Ontario

Guide · Published 26 November 2026 · 5 min read · AML Canada

Commissioner of oaths vs notary public comes down to powers. In Ontario, a commissioner for taking affidavits can administer oaths and take affidavits, declarations and affirmations. A notary public can do all of that and can also witness and attest signatures and certify true copies, which matters for documents going abroad.

Key points

  • In Ontario, a notary public has all the powers of a commissioner for taking affidavits, plus the power to attest signatures and certify true copies.
  • Every Ontario lawyer and paralegal is a commissioner automatically, but no one is a notary by virtue of office. A lawyer must still apply to be appointed.
  • Ontario's Official Documents Services won't authenticate or apostille a document commissioned by a commissioner for taking affidavits.
  • Other provinces set their own rules. Newfoundland and Labrador says its commissioners can't take affidavits for use outside the province.

What does a commissioner of oaths do in Ontario?

A commissioner for taking affidavits, often called a commissioner of oaths, takes affidavits and declarations by asking you to swear or affirm that a document is true. In Ontario, commissioners are governed by the Commissioners for Taking Affidavits Act, according to the Ontario government. An affidavit is a written statement confirmed by oath or affirmation, often used as evidence in court.

Section 9(1) of the Act requires every oath and declaration to be taken in the physical presence of the commissioner, unless the regulations allow otherwise. Section 9(3) requires the commissioner to be satisfied that the signature is genuine before signing the jurat. Section 10 of the Act makes it an offence, with a fine of up to $2,000, for a commissioner to sign a jurat or declaration without duly administering the oath or declaration. The Ministry of the Attorney General's guide for commissioners also covers this offence.

A commissioner checks who is swearing and that they understand the document. The ministry says commissioners do this without verifying whether the facts in it are accurate.

What can a notary public do in Ontario?

Under section 3 of the Notaries Act, an Ontario notary public may witness or certify, and attest, the execution of a document, certify and attest a true copy of a document, and exercise the powers of a commissioner for taking affidavits. The Ministry of the Attorney General lists these powers, subject to any restrictions in the notary's appointment.

A notary must be physically present with the person when notarizing. Remote notarization isn't permitted under the Notaries Act, and the ministry says documents notarized remotely won't be authenticated or apostilled by Ontario's Official Documents Services. Every Ontario notary uses an embossed notarial seal showing "Notary Public" and "Ontario". Non-lawyer notaries also use an approved stamp.

Is a notary public the same as a commissioner for taking oaths in Ontario?

No. A notary public holds every power a commissioner has, but a commissioner doesn't hold a notary's powers. The ministry's comparison of the two roles says commissioners can't notarize documents, notarize their own affidavit or certify true copies. Who qualifies as a commissioner of oaths in Ontario?

People holding certain offices, including lawyers, paralegals and municipal clerks, are commissioners automatically and don't apply. Anyone else must apply to the Ministry of the Attorney General and work at a registered Ontario business or organization that needs commissioner services as part of its regular business, according to the ministry's application page.

Notary status is never automatic. Ontario lawyers and paralegals licensed by the Law Society of Ontario apply to Official Documents Services and, once approved, hold a lifetime appointment while they stay in good standing. Other applicants must show that notarizing is a required part of their job, and the ministry doesn't grant a notary appointment to someone who only needs a commissioner's powers. As of 26 November 2026, the ministry lists a $75 fee for a new commissioner application and $110 for a new non-lawyer notary application. Ontario lists a $145 one-time fee for lawyers and paralegals.

Can a commissioner of oaths notarize documents in Ontario?

No. The Ministry of the Attorney General says commissioners cannot notarize documents or certify true copies. Its guide adds that the Commissioners for Taking Affidavits Act doesn't itself authorize commissioners to certify true copies, though another legal document may give a particular commissioner that authority.

Global Affairs Canada takes the same line nationally. Its authentication FAQ says the rules are set by each province or territory, and that commissioners for oaths in Canada generally aren't authorized to certify copies. Our guide to certified true copies in Canada explains who can certify a copy for different recipients.

Which one do you need for a document going abroad?

For a document that needs an apostille from Ontario, you usually need a notary. Ontario's Official Documents Services lists documents commissioned by a commissioner for taking affidavits among those it cannot authenticate. It authenticates notarized documents by comparing the notary's signature and seal with its records, and both must appear on the same page.

Some Ontario public documents, such as business registry documents issued since 1991, can be apostilled without notarizing. Global Affairs Canada says the destination country's law decides whether authentication is needed, and that a document notarized in Ontario goes to Ontario whatever its origin. Before booking, check:

  • what the foreign recipient asks for: a sworn affidavit, a witnessed signature or a certified copy;
  • whether the document can go to the competent authority without notarizing, using the official eligibility portal;
  • which province the notary is appointed in, since that largely decides where the apostille comes from;
  • that the notary will meet you in person, as Ontario won't apostille a remote notarization.

Our guides to apostille vs notarization and apostilles in Ontario cover the next steps, and our general guide explains how to get an apostille in Canada.

How other provinces draw the line

Each province sets its own rules, so the Ontario answer doesn't carry over. In Nova Scotia, the Minister of Justice appoints commissioners of oaths under the Notaries and Commissioners Act, and notary appointments are limited to MLAs and practising lawyers. Nova Scotia says a notary may certify true copies, and that another province's law may require a notary for an affidavit used there.

Newfoundland and Labrador says its commissioners for oaths cannot verify true copies, cannot take affidavits or statutory declarations for use outside the province, and cannot issue deeds and contracts. Its notary policy restricts appointments to lawyers and articled clerks in good standing, apart from cases of public need, and notaries there are appointed for life.

Where to find a notary or commissioner in Ontario

Where you go depends on what the document is for. Ontario says court affidavits can be sworn before a court official at the courthouse where you file, and ServiceOntario centres that offer commissioner services can commission affidavits needed for Ontario government services. ServiceOntario doesn't commission oaths for certain legal, financial or federal purposes. Municipal licence and zoning affidavits can be sworn before a clerk at city hall.

For private and commercial documents, you need a notary or commissioner of your own choosing. Official Documents Services says it has no in-house notary. For corporate documents going abroad, our apostille and notarization service coordinates the process, with notarization by a licensed Ontario partner notary. AML Canada isn't a notary or commissioner itself.

Can a lawyer notarize documents in Ontario?

Only if appointed as a notary. Every Ontario lawyer and paralegal is a commissioner for taking affidavits automatically, but the Ministry of the Attorney General says there are no notaries by virtue of office and lawyers must apply to be appointed.

Can a commissioner of oaths certify a true copy in Ontario?

No. The ministry says commissioners cannot certify true copies, and the Commissioners for Taking Affidavits Act doesn't itself give them that power. A notary public can certify and attest a true copy under section 3 of the Notaries Act.

Can documents be commissioned or notarized remotely in Ontario?

An oath or declaration can be commissioned remotely if the conditions in Regulation 431/20 are met. Notarization can't be done remotely under the Notaries Act, and Ontario won't apostille a remotely notarized document.

Will Ontario apostille an affidavit sworn before a commissioner?

No. Ontario's Official Documents Services lists documents commissioned by a commissioner for taking affidavits among the documents it cannot authenticate.

Does ServiceOntario notarize documents?

Ontario says ServiceOntario centres that offer commissioner services can commission affidavits needed for Ontario government services. Official Documents Services says it has no in-house notary.

The apostille vs notary question comes down to who signs what. A notary public witnesses a signature, takes an oath or certifies a copy. An apostille is a certificate from a government competent authority that confirms that signature and seal, so the document can be used in another Apostille Convention country.

Certified true copy in Canada: who can certify and when you need a notary

A certified true copy is a photocopy that an authorized person has compared with the original and marked as a true copy. Canada has no single list of who may certify one. IRCC, Service Canada and Public Safety Canada each publish their own list, the Passport Program certifies passport copies itself, and Global Affairs Canada accepts notary-certified copies for authentication. In practice, a copy going abroad with an apostille usually needs a notary.

How to get an apostille in Ontario

An Ontario apostille is issued by Official Documents Services (ODS), the provincial office that authenticates documents for use outside Canada. ODS handles documents issued in Ontario and documents notarized by an Ontario notary public. Original federal documents go to Global Affairs Canada.
